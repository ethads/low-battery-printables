How to have the awkward conversation you've been dodging for months

There's a message you've drafted in your head about forty times. Maybe it's asking a friend for the money they owe you. Maybe it's telling your mum that no, you won't be hosting again this year. Maybe it's the apology you know you owe and keep not sending. You know roughly what you want to say. You just never say it.

Avoiding these isn't a character flaw. A hard conversation is an open loop with an unpredictable other person at the end of it, and your brain treats that as a threat. The trick is to take the improvising out. If the words already exist, the conversation stops being a performance and becomes a small task.

Why this is harder than it looks

The hard part is rarely the first sentence. It's the second one, the one you have to say after they react. Most people rehearse the opener and then freeze when the other person pushes back, changes the subject or gets upset. So the conversation drifts, and you leave without having said the thing.

The fix is to know four things before you start: how you'll open, the one sentence you're there to say, what you'll say when they push back, and how you'll end it. Four lines. That's a script.

The week, one job a day

One conversation a day, by message or voice, your choice. Nothing takes more than twenty minutes. Miss a day and you do it the next one; the week can be nine days long.

  1. Asking for money back. State the amount and a date. Don't apologise for asking.
  2. Saying no to a favour. A clear no with no fake excuse attached. Offer a smaller alternative only if you actually mean it.
  3. Pushing back at work. A deadline or a load that isn't realistic. Name the trade-off and ask them to choose.
  4. A boundary with family. Say what you will and won't do, once, kindly, and stop explaining.
  5. The apology you owe. Name what you did, say sorry without a "but", and ask what would help.
  6. A complaint, finally. The thing that's gone wrong and what you'd like done about it. Facts, not feelings.
  7. Leaving a service. The gym, the provider, the subscription that keeps you on hold. One sentence, repeated as many times as needed.

The words to say

Asking for money back, by message:

"Hey, no stress, just tidying up my finances. Could you send the $80 from the dinner back by Friday? Ta."

If they say they're short this month: "No worries. What date works? I'll put it in my calendar so I don't have to chase."

Saying no to a favour:

"I can't do the airport run on Saturday, sorry. Hope it goes smoothly."

If they push: "I know it's a pain. It's still a no from me this time."

Notice there's no essay. The shorter the message, the less there is to argue with.

What counts as done

Done is that the words left your mouth or your phone. It is not the other person agreeing, apologising or being pleased with you. Their reaction is theirs. If you asked for the money and they went quiet, you still did the job. If you said no and they sulked, you still did the job. A hard conversation you've had is finished, whatever happens next.
