The school emailed about a form three weeks ago. The dentist sent a reminder you meant to ring back. There's a permission slip somewhere, and a pair of PE shorts that no longer fit, and a nursery number you have to look up every single time. You're not disorganised. You're doing a second job on four hours' sleep, and the job is admin.

Nobody tells you this part when you have a child. The feeding and the sleeping you expected. The forms, the dates, the kit lists and the constant small deadlines are the thing that actually eats the week.

Why this is harder than it looks

Parent admin arrives in pieces, all day, from five directions: a note in the bag, a text from another parent, an app notification, an email you half read. Each piece is tiny, so none of them ever feels worth stopping for, and then forty tiny pieces are overdue at once.

It also never finishes. There's no point where the forms are done, so the goal can't be to get on top of it once. The goal is a small set of fixed places where everything lands, and one short routine that empties them.

The week, one job a day

Seven sessions, twenty minutes each. Miss a day, do it the next; the week can be nine days long and that's fine.

  1. The contacts page. Every number the school or nursery might need from you, and every number you might need from them: office, absence line, class teacher's name, after-school club, the other parents you'd actually call. One page, on the fridge or in your phone.
  2. The forms pile. Gather every paper form and every form-shaped email into one spot. Do the ones that take under two minutes now. Write the rest on a list with their dates. Don't finish them all; just know what they are.
  3. One calendar. Pick one. Phone, wall, whatever you already look at. Put in term dates, inset days, clubs and the recurring stuff. Everything else in the house that holds dates gets retired.
  4. Health dates. Dentist, optician, immunisations, any specialist. Find when they were last done, book the ones that are due, put the rest in the calendar.
  5. The kit audit. Open the wardrobe and the PE bag. What fits, what's missing, what has a name label and what doesn't. Make one list. Order or label the top three things.
  6. The backup plan. Who has the kid when you're ill, when school closes, when the car won't start. Two names per situation, and they've agreed. Write it down where your partner or a grandparent could find it.
  7. The Sunday ten. Ten minutes every Sunday: check the bag, check the calendar, check the forms list. This is the routine that stops the pile re-forming.

The words to say

The backup plan means asking people, which is the bit that gets put off:

> "Can I put you down as my emergency backup for the kids? It'd only be if I'm ill or stuck. I'd do the same for you."

For the school, when a form is already late:

> "Hi, I think I've missed the deadline for the trip form. Is it still possible to send it in, and is there anything else outstanding for [child's name]?"

Schools deal with late forms every day. The second question saves you three more emails.

What counts as done

Done is one contacts page, one calendar that gets looked at, a list of outstanding forms with dates on it, and two people who've said yes to being backup. The kit list can be half done. The health dates can be booked but not attended. If the Sunday ten happens even twice, the system is working.
