A new job is admin, names and anxiety for a month. Everyone else seems to know where the good mugs live and how to log a leave request. You're nodding along in meetings while quietly trying to remember if the tall one is Priya or Preeti. By Friday you're exhausted and you haven't done any actual work yet.

If you've been meaning to get on top of it and instead just surviving each day, that's not a failing. The first month asks you to learn a hundred small things with no system for learning them. Give it a system and it gets manageable fast.

Why this is harder than it looks

There's no onboarding for the bits that matter most. Nobody schedules "learn everyone's names" or "work out which three systems you'll actually use". So those things get learnt by accident, slowly, or not at all. Meanwhile the paperwork sits half-done because it's spread across six emails and two portals.

The other hidden problem is that the first fortnight sets patterns. If you answer messages at nine at night in week one, you've set a boundary you didn't mean to.

The week, one job a day

Seven sessions spread across your first month, none over twenty minutes. Miss one and you do it the next day.

  1. Days 1 to 3, the paperwork. Make one list of everything HR, payroll and IT have asked for. Tax forms, bank details, super, ID, emergency contact. Work the list once instead of reacting to each email.
  2. Week 1, the names sheet. One page, updated daily. Name, role, something to remember them by. Nobody needs to know it exists.
  3. Week 1, the ten questions. Write down ten things you don't understand yet and ask them early, while being new is still the explanation. Asking in week one makes you look sharp; asking in month three makes you look lost.
  4. Week 2, the systems map. Which tools matter, where things live, who to ask when they break. Sketch it on one page.
  5. Week 2 or 3, run your own one-to-one. Don't wait to be given one. Book twenty minutes with your manager and bring three questions about what good looks like in this role.
  6. Week 2 or 3, set the boundaries early. Decide your hours, your response times and your lunch. Say them out loud once, before they get set for you.
  7. Day 30, the day-30 note. A short message to your manager: what you've picked up, what you've noticed, what you'd like to focus on next.

The words to say

Booking the one-to-one:

"Hi Dana, could I grab twenty minutes with you this week? I'd like to check I'm focusing on the right things and ask a couple of questions about how the team works."

The day-30 note:

"Hi Dana, I've been here a month so I wanted to send a quick check-in. I've got comfortable with the ticketing system and the Monday reporting, and I've got a decent map of who does what. I'd like to get more involved in the client onboarding work next. Anything you'd like me to adjust?"

Neither one needs to be clever. They just need to be sent.

What counts as done

Done is: the paperwork list is complete, the names sheet exists, you've asked your ten questions, you've drawn the systems map, you've had one one-to-one you booked yourself, you've said your boundaries out loud, and the day-30 note has been sent. Not feeling fully settled yet is normal and isn't on the list.
