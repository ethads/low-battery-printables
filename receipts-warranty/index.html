Somewhere in your home there is a drawer, a bag or a shoebox full of receipts. Coffee slips, supermarket receipts, three that have faded to blank, and exactly one that matters: the proof of purchase for the washing machine that has just started leaking.

You cannot tell which is which, so you keep everything, and the drawer will not shut. Or you throw everything away and then need one. Neither feels good.

What is worth keeping

Not much. Most receipts are for things you have used up or will never return. The ones that matter are for things that could cost you money if they went wrong: appliances, phones, laptops, furniture, tools, anything with a guarantee or a long life. Those are your keepers.

A smaller group is worth a quick photo: clothes you might return, a gift you are unsure about, anything you might claim on later. The rest can go in the bin without any guilt.

Three piles and a camera

Set a fifteen minute timer. Tip the drawer out on a table.

  1. Make three piles. Keep, photo then bin, and bin. Add a fourth pile if you must, called "unsure". Keep it small.
  2. Bin first. Coffees, groceries, anything used up. This is the quickest win and it clears a lot of space.
  3. Photograph the medium things. Take a clear photo of each receipt on a plain surface. Check you can read the date, the shop and the amount. Then recycle the paper.
  4. Keep the big receipts in a single envelope. Write what is in it on the front. Better still, photograph those too, so you have a backup.
  5. Make a phone album called RECEIPTS. Every photo goes in. Searching a date later is much quicker than rummaging in a drawer.
  6. Start the warranty list. For each big item, write the name, the purchase date, the shop and roughly when the guarantee ends. If you do not know, write "check".
  7. Photograph the serial number. On the back or underside of the item, there is usually a sticker with a model and serial number. Take a picture. It speeds up any claim.

That is the whole system. No filing cabinet, no colour codes.

When something breaks

You now know where the proof is, which is half the battle. Here are words to use. They are a polite request, not a legal claim.

To the shop or the maker:

"Hi, I bought [item] from you on [date]. It's stopped working. I have the receipt. Could you tell me what the next step is for a repair, replacement or refund?"

If they say the guarantee has run out:

"I understand. Given it's only [age] old, could you check whether it's still covered by anything else, or offer a goodwill repair?"

Rights differ between countries, so if the answer is no and you think it should be yes, look at what your local consumer information service says. Keep a copy of every reply, since a written reply is your best record.

No receipt? Do not panic

If a receipt has vanished, a bank or card statement showing the purchase, or the shop's confirmation email, can often serve as proof. Search your email for the shop's name before you give up. Many shops can also look up a purchase from a loyalty card or email address.

Keep it small going forward

From today, any receipt for something big gets photographed in the shop or when it arrives, before it goes in a bag. Get paper receipts into the envelope the same day. Once or twice a year, spend ten minutes clearing out anything past its guarantee.

What counts as done

Done is a bin bag filled, a few photos in the album and a short warranty list. Write down how many receipts you kept and how many you binned. You do not need to organise anything else.
