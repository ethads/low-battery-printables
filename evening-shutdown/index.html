How to switch off at night when your head keeps replaying everything

A ten-minute shutdown routine that empties unfinished worries onto paper and sets up tomorrow, so bedtime is quieter. Words for when worry returns.

You turn off the light, and the day starts again. The reply you forgot. The thing you said in the meeting. The appointment you have not booked. Tomorrow's list, read aloud by a very awake voice in the back of your head.

This is common, and it is not a sign that you are doing sleep wrong. Your brain treats unfinished things as open loops, and an open loop keeps sending reminders. The quiet of bedtime is the only time there is nothing else to drown them out.

Why lying there and trying harder does not work

If you try to forget a thought, you tend to remember it more. And if you tell yourself that you will remember it in the morning, your brain does not believe you, so it keeps rehearsing. The loop only closes when the thing has been written somewhere your brain trusts.

That is all a shutdown routine is: a short, regular ritual in which you hand the day's loose ends to paper. It does not solve any of them. It just proves to your head that they are safe.

A ten-minute shutdown

Do this about an hour before bed, or whenever you are starting to wind down. Use a plain sheet of paper. Set a timer for ten minutes and stop when it rings.

1. Empty your head (four minutes). Draw four boxes and write whatever is looping:

  • Still worrying: the thoughts that keep returning. One line each. You cannot fix them in bed, so just name them.
  • Unsent: any message, reply or call you owe someone. Names only. No drafting.
  • Tomorrow's one: the single most important thing for tomorrow. Just one.
  • Can wait: everything else that nags. It is allowed to wait until later in the week.

Write messily. Nobody will read it but you.

2. Set up tomorrow (four minutes). Do the quick jobs that make tomorrow's first hour easier:

  • Put the phone on charge, ideally outside the bedroom.
  • Check the calendar for the first appointment.
  • Lay out clothes and pack the bag.
  • Put tomorrow's one thing where you will see it first, such as on top of your keys or on the kettle.

3. Close it (two minutes). Read the page once. Then say, out loud or in your head: "Everything I'm holding is now on paper. I can pick it up in the morning." Put the page somewhere you will see it after you wake up, and put the pen down.

If a worry jumps back in

It will, at least for the first week. When it does, do not argue with it. Say: "That's on the page. I've got it. It can wait until morning."

If it is a brand new worry that is not on the page, keep a pad and a pen by the bed. Write one line, put the pen down, and say the same sentence. A new thought on paper is a closed loop, even if you do not know what to do about it yet.

If you cannot switch off at all

If twenty minutes pass and you are wide awake, get up. Sit somewhere dim, read something dull, have a drink of water, and go back to bed when you are sleepy. Lying in bed worrying about sleep teaches your brain to link the bed with worry.

Screens are worth keeping out of this hour. Not because of a rule, but because a bright, interesting thing is the opposite of a signal that the day is over.

What counts as done

Done is not "I slept well". You cannot control that, and measuring it makes it harder. Done is three small things:

  • The page is written.
  • The phone is charging.
  • You are in bed, or on your way.

That is the whole job. Whatever happens next is the sleep, and it is not your test to pass.

When this is bigger than a routine

A shutdown routine helps with ordinary worry and a busy mind. If you are not sleeping most nights, if your worries feel overwhelming, or if low mood or anxiety is affecting your days, please talk to your doctor. A sleep problem that lasts is worth a conversation, and a piece of paper is not meant to do that job.
