You've said "I should probably start looking" to at least three people. You've opened the job site, scrolled, felt sick, and closed it. The CV is somewhere, last touched for a role you no longer have. You want a new job. You just can't make yourself begin.

That isn't laziness and it isn't a lack of ambition. Job hunting is a vague cloud of dread with no edges. There's no obvious first step, every step involves being judged, and the reward is weeks away. Of course your brain avoids it. The fix is to give it edges: seven short sessions, each with one output.

Why this is harder than it looks

Most people try to do everything for one job at once. Find a listing, rewrite the CV for it, write a cover letter from scratch, panic about referees, submit, collapse. Then they have to do all of that again for the next one. No wonder most people send one application and stop.

The sprint works the other way round. Build the reusable pieces first, then send three in one sitting. The third application takes a fraction of the time the first one would have.

The week, one job a day

Nothing here takes more than twenty minutes. Miss a day and you do it the next one; the week can be nine days long.

  1. The twenty-minute CV. Don't rewrite it. Update the dates, add the current role, cut anything older than ten years to one line, and fix the formatting. Good enough beats perfect and unsent.
  2. The one paragraph. Who you are, what you do, what you're looking for. Four or five sentences. You'll paste this into every application, profile and email for the rest of the search.
  3. The tracker. A simple table: company, role, date applied, link, status. Thirty seconds per job. It stops the "did I apply to that one?" spiral.
  4. The cover letter template. One letter with three blanks: the company name, why this role, one relevant thing you did. Everything else stays the same.
  5. Message the referees. Two or three people. Ask, tell them what you're applying for, and send them your one paragraph so they know what to say.
  6. Send three. Pick three listings, fill the blanks, send, log them in the tracker. One sitting. Don't read them back more than once.
  7. The follow-up habit. A week after each send, one short message to check in. Almost nobody does this, which is exactly why it works.

The words to say

Asking someone to be a referee:

"Hi Mark, I'm starting to apply for project coordinator roles and I'd love to list you as a referee if you're comfortable with it. I've attached a short paragraph about what I'm going for so you've got the context. No rush, and thanks either way."

The follow-up, a week later:

"Hi, I applied for the project coordinator role on the 9th and wanted to check it reached you. I'm still very interested and happy to provide anything else you need. Thanks for your time."

Both are polite, short, and take under two minutes to send.

What counts as done

Done is three applications sent and logged, a CV that's current, one paragraph you can reuse, a cover letter template with blanks, and referees who know you're looking. Done is not an interview or an offer. Those happen on someone else's timetable. Your part of the job finishes when the applications leave your hands and the follow-ups are in your calendar.
