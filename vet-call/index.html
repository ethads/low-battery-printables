What to say when you ring the vet about a sick pet and your mind goes blank

First, the short safety bit. If your animal is struggling to breathe, collapsing, bleeding heavily, has had a seizure, or has eaten something poisonous, don't read this. Ring the vet or an emergency clinic now. Everything below is for the quieter worries: the limp that won't go, the scratching, the off-colour week.

You have been watching your animal for days. Eating less, maybe. Sleeping more. Something is off, and you're hoping it will pass. You haven't rung because the call asks you to explain it all to a stranger, in the right order, while you are worried. That is a lot to ask of anyone.

Why this one is harder

With your own health you can shrug and put it off. With a pet, the guilt arrives early. Every extra day feels like your fault, which makes the call heavier, which makes you put it off, and the loop tightens.

The loop breaks when the explaining is done before you dial. The vet's questions are predictable, so you can answer them on paper first.

Two minutes of notes

Before you pick up the phone, write down:

  1. What's wrong. Plain words. "Limping on the back left leg." "Scratching until the skin is red." "Not interested in food since Sunday."
  2. Since when. A rough guess is fine.
  3. Eating, drinking and toilet. Normal, less, more or none? Vets nearly always ask, and it helps them judge how soon to see you.
  4. The basics. Name, species or breed, age, and any medicine they take. Read it off the box if you need to.

If you can, take a short phone video of the problem. A limp or a cough shows better than it describes. You can show the vet or the nurse when you arrive.

The words to say

Booking in:

"Hi, I'd like to book [name] in. She's a seven year old Labrador. She's been limping on her back left leg since Sunday."

If they ask how urgent it is:

"She's still eating and drinking, but I'd like her seen soon. What's your earliest slot?"

If you're worried about cost:

"Could you give me a rough idea of the cost for the exam, and what else might be needed?"

That last one is a normal question. Clinics are used to it, and many will explain the likely costs and options before you commit to anything.

If you can't get through

Many clinics are busy in the morning. Try a different time, check whether there is an online form or message line, or ring a second practice. Keep the number of an out-of-hours emergency clinic in your phone too, and do it today while you're calm. You will be glad of it one night.

What counts as done

Done is not a diagnosis. Done is "I rang the first clinic on the list". If you book, put the date and time in your phone before you hang up, and set an alarm for the day before. If it's voicemail, leave your name, number, your pet's name and the one-line problem, then mark it tried.

If things get worse before the appointment, that is a reason to ring again and say so. Changes are exactly what they want to hear about.

Be kind to yourself

You noticed something was off. That is what a good owner does. Ringing a day later than you'd like is not neglect, and a small thing caught now is cheaper and kinder than a big thing next month.
