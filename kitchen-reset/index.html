How to clean the kitchen when it's a disaster and you have no energy

The kitchen has got away from you. Every surface has something on it, the sink is full, there's a smell you're not investigating, and making a cup of tea means moving three things first. So you don't make the tea. You order food, eat it somewhere else, and the kitchen gets a little worse.

This isn't a hygiene problem or a character flaw. A kitchen is the room that makes the most mess per hour of use, and it's the one that needs doing every single day, which is exactly the kind of task that falls off a low-battery brain. Depression, ADHD, burnout, a bad month: they all land in the kitchen first. And once it's past a certain point, "clean the kitchen" stops being a task and becomes a wall.

Why this is harder than it looks

The word "clean" is the problem. Clean means everything. Clean means the oven and the fridge shelves and the thing behind the toaster. Your brain hears "clean the kitchen", estimates three hours, and declines.

The other problem is order. If you start with the surfaces, you've got nowhere to put the dishes. If you start with the dishes, the sink is full of mouldy water. Most people start wherever their eye lands, hit a blocker in two minutes, and stop.

So change the goal. The goal is not clean. The goal is usable, meaning "I can make tea and a meal". There's a real difference between the two, and usable takes fifteen minutes if you do the steps in the right order.

The fifteen-minute version

Set a timer for fifteen minutes. Put on something to listen to. Do these four things in this order, because each one makes the next one easier.

  1. Bin, three minutes. Grab a bin bag and walk the room. Packaging, takeaway boxes, anything that's clearly rubbish. Don't wash anything, don't sort anything. Just bin. Tie the bag and put it by the door.
  2. Sink, five minutes. Empty the sink. Scrape plates into the bin. If you have a dishwasher, load it and start it. If not, wash what fits in one go and leave the rest stacked neatly to one side. The sink being empty is the turning point of the whole job.
  3. One surface, four minutes. Pick the one surface you actually use. The bit by the kettle, or the stretch where you chop. Clear it completely and wipe it. One. Not all of them.
  4. Floor, three minutes. A quick sweep or a once-over with a dustpan for the obvious bits. Not a mop. Just so you're not stepping on crumbs.

When the timer rings, stop. The kitchen is not clean. It is usable. You can make tea and a meal in it, and that was the whole point.

Keeping it usable

Two things stop you ending up back here next week.

The first is a tally on the fridge door. Mon to Sun, one tick for each day you do the fifteen minutes. Three ticks in a week counts as a reset. A row of ticks is weirdly motivating, far more than it has any right to be.

The second is the ten-minute food answer. On a good day, write down three things you can always make from what's in the house in ten minutes. Eggs on toast. Pasta with a jar of sauce. Beans. Tape the list to the fridge. On a low day you read the list instead of deciding, and deciding is the part that was stopping you eating.

What counts as done

Done is a usable kitchen, not a clean one. The bin is tied. The sink is empty. One surface is clear. The floor isn't crunchy. If you did those four things you're finished, and the oven can wait for a different kind of day. Then there's one last step, and it's the whole reason you did the rest. Fill the kettle. Put it on. That's the reset finished.
