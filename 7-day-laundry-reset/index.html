How to get through a mountain of laundry when you can't keep up with it

A week of small laundry jobs for people whose basket has become a mountain, with a system that survives a low-energy day and no folding required.

Somewhere in your house there are wet clothes in a machine, a basket that hasn't been emptied since the last season, and a chair of clean-ish things you keep re-sniffing. You've told yourself you'll do a big laundry day. You haven't, because a big laundry day is a lot.

Here is the part that isn't said enough: laundry is a chain, not a task. Gather, sort, wash, move, dry, fold, put away. Break any link and the whole thing stops, and the pile grows. Most people who "can't do laundry" can do every step. The problem is that one of them quietly fails, usually the same one every time.

The link that breaks

For many people it is the move from washer to dryer. The load finishes, you don't hear it, and by the time you remember it smells damp and needs washing again. That one failure can eat a whole weekend.

The second most common is the clean pile. Clothes get washed, dried and dumped in a basket, and the basket becomes the wardrobe. Nothing is dirty, nothing is put away.

A good system doesn't ask you to be better at remembering. It puts an alarm on the weak link.

The week, one job a day

Twenty minutes maximum. Folding is optional all week.

  1. Monday: find the piles. Gather every dirty item into one place and count the loads. A number is less scary than a mystery.
  2. Tuesday: one load. Wash the one you need most. Start it, then set a phone alarm for when it ends. Name it MOVE IT.
  3. Wednesday: move it. When the alarm rings, go straight to the machine. Move the load to the dryer, a rack, or a few doors.
  4. Thursday: put the clean pile away. Sort into drawer, hang, and bathroom or bedding. Unfolded counts.
  5. Friday: set up the system. A dirty basket where you actually undress. Two small laundry days a week, set as alarms.
  6. Saturday: the special items. Wool, delicates, the ones with scary labels. Each gets a plan, even if the plan is "bag by the door".
  7. Sunday: write the weekly plan. Your days, where the baskets live, and your low-day minimum.

What to do with the smelly load

If something has sat damp for days, a second wash on a hot cycle usually rescues it. A splash of white vinegar helps with the smell. If it's still not right, it is clothes, not a moral failing. Wash it again.

The low-day minimum

Decide it now, while you feel okay: one load started, with the MOVE IT alarm set. That's all. On a bad day you don't have to decide what to do, you read the line and do that.

Two loads a week is usually plenty for one person, and much easier to keep than one huge weekly session. A basket only gets enormous when nobody has a day for it.

Words you might need

If you share a machine:

"I'm using the machine for one load from now until [time]. Could you hold off until then?"

If you share a space and want to agree days:

"We each have a basket and a machine day. Mine are Tuesday and Saturday. Which days suit you?"

At the dry cleaner, for the awkward items:

"Hi, I'd like these cleaned please. Could you tell me when they'll be ready and how I'll be notified?"

What counts as done

Done on Tuesday is one load started and an alarm set. Done on Thursday is a clean pile that lives in drawers, or at the very least in one tidy basket. By Sunday, done is a system on one page that you wrote yourself. It doesn't have to be pretty or even folded. It has to be repeatable on your worst day.

Folding is a nice extra for a day with more energy. Plenty of people never do it and are perfectly well dressed.

When it slips

It will. Everyone's system does. Go back to Monday and count the loads. The count tells you how big the problem really is, and it's nearly always smaller than the dread.
