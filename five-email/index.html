There is an email in your inbox that has been there for three weeks. Maybe it is from a client, a landlord, a relative, a friend who asked how you were. You know what it says. You know roughly what you want to reply. And every time you open the inbox, the tiny drop in your stomach gets a little bigger.

That drop is not about the email. It is about the reply you imagine writing. In your head it is a long, careful, perfectly worded message that explains why you took so long and covers every possible question. Nobody can write that on a tired Tuesday. So the email waits, and the reply grows.

Short replies are good replies

Here is the thing most people on the other end feel when they receive a reply: relief. They do not want an essay. They want an answer. "Yes", "No", "Thursday works" or "I'll get back to you by Friday" is a complete, polite reply.

The reply you are avoiding is probably two or three lines. You just have not given yourself permission to send two or three lines.

The five-email method

Set a twenty minute timer. The job is five emails, not the whole inbox.

  1. Pick the oldest one first. Pick the one you have felt guilty about longest. Do it while you are freshest. Everything else gets easier once the worst one is gone.
  2. Pick one quick one. Find an email where a single word is the answer. A yes, a no, a thanks, a date.
  3. Pick one where someone is waiting on you. Work, admin or a form. This is the one with real consequences, and a short reply usually unblocks the other person.
  4. Pick a kind note to a friend. A message you never sent. "Thinking of you" is a whole message.
  5. Pick one more. Anything. Do not overthink it.
  6. Write the answer in one line before you write the email. On a scrap of paper: who, what they want, and your answer. "Yes, Thursday." "No, not this time." "I'll tell them by Friday." Then paste it into the email.

When the fifth one is sent, close the inbox. The sixth is tomorrow's.

Words you can steal

The hardest part is the opening, so here are openings.

If you are late:

"Hi [name], thanks for your email and sorry for the slow reply. The answer is [yes, no, not this time]."

If you need more time:

"Hi [name], I've got this and I'll get back to you by [day]."

If you cannot do all of it:

"Thanks for this. I can't do [the big thing] but I can do [a smaller thing]."

To a friend:

"I'm so sorry I went quiet. I read your message and then life got loud. I'd love to catch up. Are you free [day]?"

You are allowed to say sorry once. After that, move on. A paragraph of apology makes the reply longer and the other person more uncomfortable.

When you cannot decide

Sometimes you cannot answer because you genuinely do not know the answer. That is fine. "Let me come back to you by Friday" is an answer, because it gives the other person a date and takes the weight off you. Put Friday in your calendar so it becomes a real job.

Send, do not proofread twice

Read it once. Fix any typo you can see. Press send. The second proofread is where the anxiety wins. A short, slightly imperfect reply that exists beats a perfect one that does not.

Once sent, archive the email. An inbox with five fewer waiting messages feels lighter than you expect.

What counts as done

Done is five replies sent, not five perfect replies. Write down how many you sent and how many are still waiting. Pick a day for the next five. If you only managed three, that is three more than yesterday.
