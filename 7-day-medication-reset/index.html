How to keep on top of your medication when you keep running out or forgetting

You reach for the box and it is empty. It is Sunday evening, the pharmacy is shut, and the clinic that has to approve the repeat does not open until Monday. Or you cannot remember whether you took today's dose, and standing in the kitchen going through the morning does not help.

Medication admin is one of those hidden jobs. It looks like a single thing, take your tablets, but it is really a chain. Know what you take. Know when you will run out. Ask for the repeat. Collect it. Remember to take it. Remember that you took it. If any one link slips, the whole thing wobbles.

This is about admin only

A note first. This is about the admin around medication. It will not tell you what to take, how much, or when to change anything. Those questions belong with your doctor or pharmacist, and you should never change a dose because of an article. What follows is how to make sure the system works around the plan you already have.

A week, one link a day

Each job is small. Miss a day and do it the next. The week can be nine days long.

  1. Monday: write the list. Copy from the boxes, not from memory. For each medication: the name, the strength, how it is written to be taken, what it is for if you know, who prescribes it and which pharmacy it comes from. Add supplements. Take a photo of the page. If a label is unclear, leave it blank and ask the pharmacist.
  2. Tuesday: count what is left. Count what is in each box, divide by how many you take a day and write the days remaining beside the name. Mark anything under ten days in red.
  3. Wednesday: request the refill. Start with the red ones. Find out how your clinic takes repeat requests, whether that is online, an app, a phone call or a slip at the pharmacy.
  4. Thursday: set the reminders. One repeating alarm for each dose time, named plainly ("Morning tablets. Water."), and one monthly reminder called "Order repeat" about ten days before the earliest run-out date.
  5. Friday: set up a pillbox. A weekly box shows at a glance whether today's dose is taken. Ask the pharmacist if any of your medication should stay in its packet.
  6. Saturday: plan for travel and bad days. A minimum supply before any trip, a back-up if you miss a refill and one person who knows where your list is.
  7. Sunday: write the one page that holds everything, and keep it where you can find it.

The words to use

To the clinic: "Hi, I'd like to request a repeat prescription for [medication, strength]. I have about [number] days left. Could you tell me when it will be ready and how I'll know?"

To the pharmacy: "Hi, I'm waiting on a repeat for [medication]. Could you check whether it has arrived and let me know when I can collect it?"

To ask about a pillbox: "Hi, I'd like to use a weekly pill organiser. Are any of my medications unsuitable for one, and is there a blister-pack service that could do this for me?"

For a missed dose, ask in advance and write the answer down: "If I forget a dose of [medication], what should I do?" Pharmacists are used to this question, and having the answer on paper means you are not guessing on the day.

Tie the dose to something you already do

Reminders work better when they are tied to a habit you already have. Put the pillbox next to the kettle, or by your toothbrush. Write "after the kettle" on the list. The alarm is the back-up, and the habit is the system.

If a refill needs a review

Some medications cannot be repeated without a check-up. If the clinic says you need an appointment, book it that day, and ask what to do in the meantime. Do not stretch your doses to make them last, or change anything yourself. Ask the doctor or pharmacist how to manage the gap.

Travel

If you are going abroad, check the rules for carrying your medication in the country you are going to, with your doctor, your pharmacist or official travel guidance, well before you go. Rules differ and can be strict.

What counts as done

On each day there is one thing: the list, the count, one refill requested, the alarms, the box filled, the plan written, the page saved. By Sunday, you have a list you can show to any professional, a reminder that goes off before you run out and a plan for the days it all goes wrong.

Next month, it is a five-minute check, not a scramble.
