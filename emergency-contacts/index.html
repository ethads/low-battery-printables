Here is a question that is uncomfortable for about five seconds and then useful for years. If something happened to you and you could not speak, would anyone know who to ring?

Most of us have the numbers in a phone that is locked, and a vague plan that someone will work it out. Often that works. Sometimes it does not, and the people who most need the information are the ones who cannot find it.

What this is, and what it is not

This is not a will, a legal document or a medical record. It is one page. It lists the people to call, the few facts that help someone looking after you, and where to find the rest. It is the kind of thing a paramedic, a neighbour or a worried friend would be very glad to find on your fridge.

Writing it is not a worry list. It is the opposite. Once it exists, you do not need to carry it in your head.

The fifteen minute version

Set a timer. You need one sheet of paper and a pen.

  1. Write your details. Full name, date of birth, home address, and the name you like to be called.
  2. Write your health notes. Allergies, regular medicines, long-term conditions, and the name of your doctor. Add your blood type if you know it. If you are unsure of something, write "ask GP" and leave it for now.
  3. Write who lives with you. Children, flatmates, pets who need feeding, a key held by a neighbour. If someone depends on you, this is the section that matters most.
  4. Write your insurance providers. Health, home, car, travel. The names are enough. Policy numbers can sit with your papers, and you can write where.
  5. List at least three people to call. In order. Choose people who are not all in the same house. Add a backup phone number or email for each.
  6. Add the local emergency number and your doctor's number at the bottom. Check the numbers using your local official source, since they differ between places.
  7. Fill in what you can and leave the rest. A half-done sheet is better than none, and blanks can be filled over the week.

Asking someone to be on your list

Do not add someone without checking. A short text is polite and enough.

"Hi [name], I'm putting together an emergency contacts sheet. Would you be OK being on it? It would just mean someone might ring you if I couldn't reach anyone."

If they say yes:

"Thank you. Can I check your best number, and a backup, in case I can't reach you?"

If someone hesitates, treat it as a gentle no. Pick someone else. It is not personal, and people have their own reasons.

Where to put it

A sheet in a drawer is no use. Put it somewhere it can be found in a hurry: the fridge, or a folder by the front door. Take a photo and save it as a favourite in your phone, or add it to a note you can open without unlocking the phone. Some phones have a medical or emergency information screen you can reach from the lock screen. Check how yours works, and add the key facts there.

Give a copy to one trusted person. Tell them where yours lives.

Keep it fresh

Phone numbers change. People move. Pick a day each year, such as your birthday, to check the sheet is still right. Two minutes, and you are done. If something big changes, such as a new medicine, a new job or a move, update it the same day.

What counts as done

Done is a sheet with your own details and at least the first name filled in, stuck on the fridge. It does not need to be complete. Write down how many people are on the list and who you still need to ask.

That is enough for today. The rest is a few texts.
