How to stop your phone running your day when you can't face a detox

Seven small settings changes over seven days, ten to twenty minutes each, that turn a phone that steals your attention into one that waits for you.

You pick the phone up to check one thing and surface forty minutes later with no memory of the forty minutes. The notifications arrive all day. The home screen is a wall of red badges. The storage warning has been there for a month. You know a detox would help and you also know you won't do one, because the phone is your alarm, your bank, your map and the way everyone reaches you.

That isn't weak will. The phone is designed by very well paid people to take your attention, and the ADHD brain is exactly the brain it works best on. Blaming yourself for losing to it is like blaming yourself for losing an arm wrestle with a machine.

Why this is harder than it looks

Digital detox advice asks you to remove the phone. That fails for the same reason every all-or-nothing plan fails: the first time you need it, the plan is over and you feel like you've lost. What actually changes things is the settings, one at a time, so the phone stops interrupting and starts waiting.

The trouble is that the settings are scattered, there are dozens of them, and each one is a small decision. Nobody does them all in one go. Done one a day, each takes ten to twenty minutes and sticks.

The week, one job a day

One setting or one clear-out a day. Ten to twenty minutes. Miss a day and do it the next one; the week can be nine days long.

  1. Monday: kill the notifications. Go through every app and turn off everything except calls, messages from people, and the calendar. Shops, games and social apps go silent.
  2. Tuesday: rebuild the home screen. First screen gets only tools: camera, maps, notes, calendar, bank. Everything that eats time moves to a folder on the second screen.
  3. Wednesday: limit the two worst. Pick the two apps that take the most hours and set a daily time limit on each. Not zero. A limit you'll actually hit.
  4. Thursday: clear the storage warning. Delete the apps you haven't opened in six months and the downloads folder. The warning goes, and so does a small daily nag.
  5. Friday: dump the photos. Back them up to wherever they live long-term, then delete the screenshots and the twelve near-identical versions of the same shot.
  6. Saturday: fix the contacts. Merge the duplicates, delete the numbers you don't recognise, add the names you've been meaning to save.
  7. Sunday: move the charger. Out of the bedroom, or at least across the room. The phone charges where you can't reach it from the pillow.

The words to say

Most of this week is between you and the settings menu, but one or two people may notice you reply slower:

"I've turned most of my notifications off, so I'll see messages when I check rather than straight away. If it's urgent, ring me."

And to yourself, when the first screen looks empty and strange on Tuesday night:

"It's supposed to look boring. Boring is the point."

What counts as done

Done on Monday is a phone that only makes a noise when a person wants you. Done on Sunday is a phone charging somewhere that isn't an arm's length from your head. In between, each day has one small done line: a home screen with only tools on it, two apps with limits, a storage warning gone, a camera roll you can scroll, contacts with names. No detox, no deleted accounts. Just a phone that waits for you instead of the other way round.
