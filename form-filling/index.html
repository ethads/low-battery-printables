How to start a government form when you've been avoiding it for weeks

A fifteen-minute way to start the benefit or government form you keep avoiding. Gather the numbers once, answer five questions, save and stop.

The form has been sitting there for weeks. You know roughly where it is. You know roughly what it wants. And every time you think about it, something in you quietly closes the door and finds another thing to do.

That is not laziness. A government form asks you to find eight pieces of information you don't have to hand, hold them all in your head, and then answer forty questions in an order you didn't choose, with a deadline attached. For an ADHD brain, or any brain on a low-energy week, that is a wall, not a task.

Why this is harder than it looks

Most advice says sit down and do it. But the form is not one job. It is three: gathering the numbers, answering the questions, and remembering where you got to. The gathering is where most people fall over, because every missing reference number sends you into a drawer, an email search or a phone call, and by the time you come back the form has timed out and you've lost the lot.

Then there's the deadline. The closer it gets, the scarier the form feels, and the scarier it feels the less you can look at it. That loop is normal. It isn't a character flaw, it's just how avoidance works, and the way out is to make the first step so small it doesn't trigger it.

The fifteen-minute version

You are not finishing the form today. You are gathering and starting. Set a timer for fifteen minutes and work through this in order.

  1. Write down, on one sheet of paper, the things every form asks for: your ID and reference numbers, your last payslip or income figure, tax and insurance references, and rough dates (when you started a job, when you moved, when the thing happened). Approximate is fine for now. You can correct it later.
  2. Keep that sheet. Every future form takes its numbers from here, so you never hunt again.
  3. List the forms you're avoiding, sorted by deadline. Circle the one due soonest.
  4. Open the circled form. Answer five questions. Just five.
  5. Save it. Write down which question you reached, next to the form on your list.
  6. Close it. You're done for today.

Five questions answered and saved is a full session. A form half done and saved beats a form done perfectly in your head.

The words to say

Helplines exist for exactly this, and the people on them answer the same questions all day. You don't need to explain yourself or apologise. If you're stuck on a question, something like this is plenty:

"Hi, I'm filling in the [form name] and I'm stuck on the question about [topic]. Can you tell me what they're actually asking for?"

If you're going to miss the date, say so before it passes rather than after:

"Hi, I've got the [form name] due on [date] and I don't think I'll make it. What happens if it's late, and is there any way to get an extension?"

And if you'd rather not do it alone, libraries, Citizens Advice and local charities will sit with you while you fill it in. Asking for that is a completely normal request:

"Could someone help me fill in a form? I've got all the paperwork, I just can't get through it on my own."

What counts as done

Done is not a submitted form. Done is the gather sheet filled in, the forms listed by date, and five questions saved on the one that's due first. If you did that, you did the hard part, which is starting. Tomorrow, or whenever the next window of energy shows up, you open the form at the question you wrote down and do five more. Three or four of those sessions and it's gone.
