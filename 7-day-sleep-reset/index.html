How to start fixing your sleep when you're tired all day and wired at night

You're tired all day. Then bedtime arrives and your brain wakes up. You scroll, you stare at the ceiling, you replay a conversation from 2019, and at some point you sleep, badly. In the morning you're wrecked, and the cycle starts again.

Most sleep advice sounds like it was written for someone with a calm evening and a tidy bedroom. If you're running on low energy, or your brain doesn't quiet down on command, a list of fifteen rules is just another thing to fail at.

So this is smaller. Seven changes, one a day, none longer than twenty minutes. It's not a cure and it's not medical advice. If your sleep has been poor for weeks, or you're worried about it, talk to a doctor. This week helps you start, and gives you something useful to bring to them.

A note before you begin

Lying awake isn't failing. Rest counts, even without sleep. You don't have to do any of this perfectly, and you don't have to score last night. You're writing it down, not grading it.

The week, one change a day

Miss a day and do it the next day. A nine-day week is fine.

  1. Monday: write down what's happening. A few plain lines each morning: bedtime, roughly when you fell asleep, how many times you woke, how you feel, and what you did in the three hours before bed. Two minutes.
  2. Tuesday: pick a wake time. One you can keep within an hour, even at weekends. It doesn't have to be early. It has to be steady. Put the alarm across the room.
  3. Wednesday: fix the room. Cooler, darker, quieter. Move the laundry out of sight. Wash the sheets if you can.
  4. Thursday: build a wind-down. Three small calming steps, in the same order, starting about half an hour to an hour before bed. Dim the lights.
  5. Friday: park the worries. Ten minutes earlier in the evening, not in bed, writing down everything on your mind, with a next step or "not tonight" beside each.
  6. Saturday: cut one thing. Look at your notes and pick the single habit you suspect is hurting, such as late caffeine, a phone in bed or a heavy late meal. Change that one.
  7. Sunday: write the plan. What helped, what you'll keep, and whether to book an appointment.

The words to say

When a thought wakes you up at night, it helps to have a line ready:

"It's on the list. I can deal with it tomorrow. Right now my job is to rest."

If you share a room or a house and the evening noise is the problem:

"Could we agree on a time to turn the lights and the TV off? It would really help me sleep."

And if you decide to see a doctor:

"Hi, I'd like an appointment about my sleep. It's been difficult for [how long], and I've kept notes for the last week."

When to talk to a doctor

It's worth booking an appointment if poor sleep has gone on for several weeks, if it's affecting your mood, work or safety, if you snore loudly or wake gasping, or if anything about it worries you. Sleep trouble can have many causes, and a doctor can look at the whole picture. A guide can't. If you're unsure whether you qualify, that's reason enough to ask.

If a small change like cutting back on caffeine gives you headaches or other effects, ease off gradually, and ask a pharmacist or doctor if you have questions.

What counts as done

Done on Monday is last night written down. Done on Wednesday is one change to the room. Done on Sunday is one page with your wake time, your wind-down and the thing you cut, plus an appointment if you need one. You won't have fixed your sleep in a week. You'll have a steadier start, a clearer picture, and some evidence, which is more than most people bring to the conversation.
