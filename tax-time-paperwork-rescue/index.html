The deadline is coming and your paperwork is in four email accounts, a kitchen drawer and a tote bag you haven't opened since last year. You know the return has to happen. You also know that the first step is finding things, and finding things is the step you can't start.

This isn't about being bad with money. Tax paperwork is a search task with no map, done once a year, under a deadline, about a subject that feels like it could punish you for getting it wrong. Almost anyone's brain refuses that. The way through is to stop thinking about the return and think only about the folder.

To be clear from the start: nothing here is tax advice. This is a document-gathering system. The return itself is for you, your accountant or a free helpline. Your job is to hand them everything in one place.

Why this is harder than it looks

Finding is harder than filing. Documents arrive across a whole year from different senders, in different formats, to whichever inbox you were using at the time. Some are emails. Some are PDFs in a downloads folder. Some are paper. Nobody puts them together as they come in, so at tax time you're reconstructing a year.

The other problem is that you don't know what you're missing until you've found what you have. That's why the order matters.

The week, one job a day

Nothing takes more than twenty minutes. Miss a day and you do it the next one; the week can be nine days long.

  1. Find the income documents. Payment summaries, invoices you sent, bank interest, anything that paid you. Search every inbox for the obvious words and drop results in one folder.
  2. Find the expense documents. Anything you might claim or ask about. You don't decide what counts; you just collect it so the person doing the return can.
  3. Pull the bank statements. Download the year's statements for every account you used. Most banks let you export the whole period in a few clicks.
  4. The receipt sweep. Empty the bag, the drawer, the car glovebox and the photos app. Photograph the paper ones. Put them all in the folder.
  5. Last year's return and the reference numbers. Find last year's return and the identifying numbers you'll need. They make everything downstream faster.
  6. The questions list. Write down everything you're unsure about. "Can I claim this?" goes on the list, not in your head. The list is for your accountant or the helpline.
  7. Hand it over, and set up next year. Send or take the folder to whoever is doing the return. Then make one folder for next year and one rule: anything tax-shaped goes in it the day it arrives.

The words to say

Handing over to an accountant:

"Hi, I've put everything for this year in one folder: income documents, expense documents, bank statements, receipts and last year's return. There's also a list of things I'm not sure about. Let me know what's missing and I'll find it."

Calling a helpline:

"Hi, I'm doing my own return and I've got my documents together. I have a short list of questions about what I can include. Is this the right place to ask?"

You're not expected to know the answers. You're expected to have the folder and the questions.

What counts as done

Done is one folder, paper or digital, with everything you could find in it, plus a questions list, handed to the person or service doing the return. Done is not a completed return and not a perfect record of every coffee. If something is missing, the person doing the return will tell you, and you'll know exactly where to look.
