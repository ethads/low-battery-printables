How to make a shopping list when the cupboards are bare and you're too tired to think

A ten-minute way to turn an empty fridge into four easy dinners and one shopping list, for days when planning meals feels like too much.

It's six in the evening and you're standing in the kitchen with the fridge door open. There is food in there, technically. But none of it adds up to dinner, the delivery slots are gone, and the thought of going to a shop and choosing things makes your chest go tight.

If this happens to you often, it isn't a cooking problem. Feeding yourself is a stack of small decisions: what do I have, what could that become, what's missing, where do I buy it, when. Each one takes a bit of working memory. On a low-battery week, the stack falls over, and you end up eating toast again.

Why the panic is worse than the problem

The panic comes from trying to plan and shop in one go. You imagine a whole week of balanced meals, and nothing in your cupboard matches it. So you do nothing, or you order takeaway you can't really afford, and tomorrow looks the same.

You don't need a week. You need four dinners that use roughly what you've got, plus a short list for the gaps. Four is enough to break the panic. The rest of the week can be cereal and eggs.

The ten-minute version

Set a timer. Open three doors and write down what you see, without judging any of it.

  1. Fridge. Name three things that are still good. Eggs, cheese, half a pepper, a tub of hummus. Just three.
  2. Freezer. Find one protein: mince, fish fingers, chicken pieces, a bag of prawns. One.
  3. Cupboard. Look for the base foods: pasta, rice, noodles, tins of beans or tomatoes, cereal.
  4. Running out. Milk, bread, loo roll, coffee. Whatever is nearly gone goes on the list now.

Now write four dinners that use those things. Keep them boring on purpose. Pasta with a jar of sauce. Eggs on toast with a bag of salad. Fish fingers, peas and potatoes. Noodles with frozen veg and an egg. Next to each dinner, write what you already have and what you'd need to buy. The "need to buy" column is your shopping list. You've just made it without ever opening a recipe site.

Add one breakfast and one lunch you can eat standing up, then stop. Planning more than that is how this turns back into a big job.

Shopping from the list

Group the list by section of the shop: fruit and veg, fridge, frozen, tins and dry goods, other. It means fewer laps and fewer chances to wander off and spend money you didn't plan to. Take a photo so the list lives on your phone.

If the shop itself is the hard part, the list still helps. You can use it for click and collect, a delivery slot, or to hand to someone who's going anyway.

If you're asking someone to pick things up:

"Hi, could you grab a few things for me on your way past? I've put the list in a message. I'll send you the money back tonight."

If you're adding to a shared household list:

"Could you add [item] to the list? Any brand is fine."

If the delivery slots are all gone, try click and collect, a different day, or a small local shop for just the three things you can't do without. Three items is a shop you can finish.

What counts as done

Done is not "I've been shopping". Done is "I have a list and I know which day I'm going". That's it. Write the day down, put it in your phone with an alarm, and close the list. If that's all you did today, it was enough, and you are already ahead of where you were an hour ago.

A few boring facts that help: eggs, pasta, rice, tinned beans and frozen veg make about ten different meals between them. Eating the same five dinners for a month is allowed. Nobody is marking you on variety, and the version of dinner that actually happens beats the version that stays in your head.
