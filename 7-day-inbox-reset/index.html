The number on the mail icon stopped meaning anything a long time ago. Somewhere in there are three emails that actually matter, buried under years of newsletters, receipts and notifications you never asked for. You open the app, feel the weight, and close it again. The three important ones stay buried.

That isn't disorganisation. An inbox with thousands of items is a room where every object is shouting, and no brain is designed to triage that. Avoiding it is the sane response. The problem is that avoidance doesn't stop new mail arriving.

Why this is harder than it looks

Inbox zero advice assumes you can process every email one at a time to a decision. With four thousand, that's weeks of work, and the method collapses on day two. What you need isn't a processing system. You need to make the room quiet first, then deal with the few things that were actually talking to you.

The other problem is replies. The emails you've left longest are the ones you feel worst about, and feeling bad about them makes them harder to answer, so they age further. The way out is a template that doesn't require you to explain the delay.

The week, one job a day

One move a day, twenty minutes. Miss a day and do it the next one; a nine-day week is still a week.

  1. Monday: the big archive. Select everything older than a month and archive it. Not delete. It's all still searchable; it just isn't shouting.
  2. Tuesday: unsubscribe. Open what's left and hit unsubscribe on every newsletter, shop and notification you don't read. Twenty minutes' worth, then stop.
  3. Wednesday: the three replies. The three you've been dreading. Short, template below, send and close.
  4. Thursday: two filters. Set up two rules that sort the most frequent senders (receipts, a mailing list) straight past your inbox.
  5. Friday: one folder. One folder for "needs action". Anything that needs you goes in it. Everything else gets read and archived.
  6. Saturday: phone notifications. Turn email notifications off on your phone entirely, or down to one quiet summary a day.
  7. Sunday: the ten-minute habit. Ten minutes, once a week: archive what's done, unsubscribe from whatever crept in, check the action folder. That's the maintenance.

The words to say

For the reply you've put off for weeks:

"Hi [name], sorry for the slow reply on this. Yes, that works for me. Let me know the next step."

For the one you've put off for months:

"Hi [name], apologies this sat so long. Is this still live? If so, I'm happy to pick it up now."

For the one you need to say no to:

"Thanks for thinking of me. I can't take this on at the moment, but I appreciate you asking."

One line of apology at most. No story. The other person almost certainly stopped waiting and will just be pleased to hear from you.

What counts as done

Done on Monday is an inbox with this month's mail in it and nothing else. Done on Wednesday is three sent replies, however short. By Sunday, done is an inbox you can open without flinching: fewer senders, two filters quietly sorting, one folder for what needs you, a phone that doesn't buzz every time a shop has a sale, and ten minutes a week keeping it that way. Not zero. Quiet.
