The invoice is forty days overdue and you still haven't sent the reminder. You've drafted it twice. It sits there because asking for money you've already earned feels like begging, and because somewhere you've decided the client will think less of you. That isn't laziness, and it isn't bad business sense. It's the one part of freelancing nobody teaches, so everyone makes it up alone and most people make it up badly.

Meanwhile the client has probably just forgotten. Late payment is rarely personal. It's usually a system that didn't flag your invoice.

Why this is harder than it looks

Chasing money involves three separate skills: making the invoice itself hard to ignore, keeping track of what's owed and when, and then having the conversation. Most freelancers only ever work on the third one, in a panic, when the rent is due. By then there's no tracker to lean on, the invoice had no terms line so there's nothing to point at, and the message has to be written from scratch while you're already upset.

The fix is to do the boring parts first, so the chase becomes a copy-and-paste rather than a composition.

The week, one job a day

Seven short sessions. Twenty minutes maximum. Miss one and do it tomorrow; a nine-day week still counts.

  1. Fix the invoice. Due date in bold, as an actual date rather than a number of days. Your payment details where they can't be missed. A reference the client's accounts team can file. The job description short enough to read.
  2. The terms line. One sentence on every invoice from now on: when payment is due and what happens if it's late. You're not being aggressive; you're being clear before it matters.
  3. The tracker. One spreadsheet or page: client, amount, date sent, date due, date chased, date paid. Fill in everything currently owed to you. Looking at the total is the hardest part of the week.
  4. Send the chases. Everything on the tracker that's overdue gets a message today, using the four-stage sequence: friendly, firm, formal, final. Match the stage to how late it is. Send them all in one sitting.
  5. The awkward three. The client who says they never got it, the client who disputes the work, the client who's gone quiet. Write your reply to each once, so you're never improvising again.
  6. Deposits, from now on. Decide your deposit percentage and add it to your proposal template. New work doesn't start until it lands.
  7. The Friday fifteen. Fifteen minutes every Friday: update the tracker, send any chases due. This is what stops the pile from ever being forty days deep again.

The words to say

The friendly stage, which is where most late invoices end:

> "Hi [name], just checking in on invoice [number] for [amount], which was due on [date]. Could you let me know when I can expect payment? Happy to resend if it's gone astray."

The firm stage, a week or two later:

> "Hi [name], invoice [number] is now [X] days overdue. I'd appreciate payment by [date]. If there's an issue with the invoice, let me know today so we can sort it."

Short, polite, no apology. You're not sorry for asking to be paid.

What counts as done

Done is a tracker that shows every unpaid invoice, every overdue one chased at the right stage, and a terms line on the template so next month's invoices are easier. You don't have to have been paid by the end of the week. Money arriving is the client's job. Asking clearly, on a schedule, is yours, and by Friday you'll have done it.
