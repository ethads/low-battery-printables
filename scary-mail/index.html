There's a pile of unopened envelopes on the side, and you know roughly what's in it. You've known for weeks. Opening one feels like volunteering for bad news, so you don't, and then the late fees arrive anyway and the pile gets a little heavier. That isn't laziness. It's a completely reasonable response to things that feel like threats, and brains that run on avoidance do it very efficiently.

The problem is that the pile doesn't get less scary with time. It gets scarier, because now it's also evidence that you've been avoiding it.

Why this is harder than it looks

Opening mail sounds like a two-minute task. It isn't, because every envelope arrives with an implied instruction: open me and then deal with me. Dealing with it might mean a phone call, a payment you can't make, or a decision you don't know how to make. So your brain treats the whole pile as one enormous task, and enormous tasks don't get started.

The trick is to break the link between opening and dealing. For ten minutes, you're only allowed to open. Fixing is forbidden. That rule is what makes it possible.

The ten-minute version

Set a timer. Ten minutes, no more.

  1. Open everything. Every envelope, every one. Don't read properly yet. Just get the paper out and the envelopes in the recycling. The pile has to stop being a pile of unknowns.
  2. Sort into four boxes. Four spots on the table, for example bin, file, needs money, needs a reply. Each letter goes in one. Don't deliberate. If you're not sure, it needs a reply.
  3. Write down the scary ones. For anything in the money or reply boxes, one line: who it's from, what they want, when it's due. That's all. Writing it down moves it from dread to information.
  4. Circle one. Look at the list and circle the single item that needs you next. Usually the soonest due date. Not the biggest, not the worst. The next one.
  5. Do the one thing, or book it. If the circled item is a five-minute job, do it now. If it needs a call you can't face right now, write a day and time next to it. That counts.
  6. Close the folder. Put the file pile in a folder, the list on top, and put it away. You're finished. Fixing nothing else today is the whole point.

Most people find the pile is about a third junk, a third things that just needed filing, and a third actual problems, and that the actual problems were smaller than the pile made them feel.

The words to say

The circled item is often a bill you can't pay in full, and the call is where people get stuck for months. You don't have to work out what to say. Something in this spirit does the job:

> "Hi, I'm calling about my account, number [X]. I've got a bill due on [date] and I can't pay the full amount right now. Can I set up a payment plan, or get an extension?"

Then stop talking and let them answer. They have a process for this; it's a normal call for them. If the phone is too much, the same words work in web chat or email:

> "I'm writing about account [X]. I can't pay the full balance by [date]. Please can you tell me what options I have for a payment plan or an extension?"

You're not asking for forgiveness. You're asking for information, and most companies would rather have a plan than a missed payment.

What counts as done

Done is every envelope open, every letter in a box, the scary ones written down with their due dates, and one thing either done or booked. The folder is closed. You haven't fixed your finances, and you weren't supposed to. You've turned a pile of threats into a short list, and a short list is something you can come back to.
