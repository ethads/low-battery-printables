How to get your kitchen and food under control when you've given up on cooking

A week of one small food job a day, twenty minutes max: clear the fridge, find what you have, write one list and set up a backup for bad days.

For a while now, food has been somewhere between a chore and a source of guilt. The fridge is a mystery. The cupboards hold things from two moves ago. Dinner is whatever's in reach, or whatever you can order, and you never quite have what you need when you need it.

Food admin is easy to get wrong because it's several jobs wearing one coat: knowing what you have, knowing what you eat, knowing what's missing, buying it, and having a plan for the days you can't cook. The usual advice is to do all of that on a Sunday with a meal prep playlist. On a low-battery week, that's a lot to ask.

Spread across a week, each job is small. Twenty minutes, one thing, then you're done.

The week, one job a day

Nothing on this list takes longer than twenty minutes, apart from the shop on Friday. Miss a day and do it the next day. A nine-day week is fine.

  1. Monday: empty the fridge of the obvious. Bin bag open, one shelf at a time, top to bottom. The only question is "would I eat this off a fork right now?" Wipe the top shelf and stop.
  2. Tuesday: sort the pantry. One shelf at a time into three piles: bin, eat soon, keep. Tins you'll never eat go to a food bank or a neighbour.
  3. Wednesday: find your five meals. Not recipes. Five things you can make half asleep from stuff you actually buy, each with its three ingredients written down.
  4. Thursday: write the one list. Whatever's missing from yesterday's meals, plus the things that always run out, grouped by section of the shop.
  5. Friday: shop once, on purpose. Eat first. Take the list. Delivery or click and collect counts. Put the cold things away, then stop.
  6. Saturday: set up the lazy backup. One shelf with three things you can heat and eat for bad days, and a sticky note that says so.
  7. Sunday: write the one page. Your five meals, the backup shelf, the three things that always run out, and a ten-minute weekly check.

The words to say

The only day that needs other people is when you ask for help with the shop, or you're clearing out the cupboards for a food bank. For the shop:

"Could you pick these up for me on your way past? I'll send you the money straight back."

For the neighbour or friend you're passing things on to:

"I'm having a clear-out. Would you like any of these tins and packets? They're all in date."

For a bad evening, say it to yourself: "I don't have to cook tonight. The backup shelf is the plan, not a failure."

Why it works

Most people find that the fridge is the emotional block and the pantry is the practical one. Clearing the fridge removes the guilt, and sorting the pantry shows you what you already have, which is usually more than you thought. Writing the five meals stops you re-deciding what to eat every evening. And the lazy backup means a bad night doesn't turn into a bad week.

None of it involves a single container, label or meal prep. The kitchen doesn't need to look good. It needs to work.

What counts as done

Done on Monday is a bag in the outside bin. Done on Wednesday is five meals on a page. Done on Friday is food in the house, however it got there. By Sunday, done is one page that tells you what you eat, what you need and what to reach for when you can't cook. That's the system, and it fits on the fridge door.
