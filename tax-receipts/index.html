It is not tax time yet, but part of you is already dreading it. You know the receipts are somewhere. A drawer, an email folder, a coat pocket, a photo you took in March of something you cannot now identify. When the day comes, you will spend an afternoon hunting and feeling behind.

You can change that, and the change is mostly one small habit plus one short session now.

Gather first, decide later

The most useful idea in tax admin is to separate gathering from deciding. Gathering is easy: you are only putting receipts in one place. Deciding is hard: you are working out what counts, what is claimable and how much. Deciding is also not your job on your own. That is a question for an accountant or your local tax office, and the rules differ widely between countries.

So today you do the gathering only. If in doubt, keep it. You can always throw it away later. You cannot get it back.

The twenty minute session

Set a timer. You need a folder, a phone album and a pen.

  1. Make four buckets. Work or business costs, giving or donations, health costs, and home or other. These are broad on purpose. You are sorting, not judging.
  2. Make a log. One line per receipt: the date, what it was, who it was from, the amount and the bucket number.
  3. Start with the biggest receipts. The three biggest are the ones you will most regret losing. Photograph each one and save it in an album called TAX.
  4. Check your email. Search for "receipt", "invoice" and "order confirmation". Forward the useful ones to a folder or label called TAX.
  5. Check your bank for gaps. Scroll through a month of statements. Anything that looks like a work cost, a donation or a medical bill and has no receipt goes on the log as "need a copy".
  6. Do not add up the amounts. You can total the log later. For now it is proof that the receipts exist.

Asking for copies

Missing a receipt is normal and usually fixable. Most shops and suppliers can send a copy in a few minutes.

To a shop or supplier:

"Hi, could you please send me a copy of the receipt or invoice for my purchase on [date]? The amount was [amount] and the order reference is [number]. Thank you."

To a charity:

"Hi, could you send me a receipt for my donation of [amount] on [date]? I'd like it for my records."

If you cannot get a receipt at all, write the date, shop and amount in your log anyway. A bank statement line can often back it up, but check what your local tax office accepts.

One habit that does the work

The session above clears the backlog. The habit keeps it clear. Whenever a receipt email arrives, forward it to the TAX folder straight away. Whenever you get a paper receipt for something that might count, photograph it before it goes in a bag. Make it a ten second job, done at the till or on the sofa.

If you have a side income or run a small business, ask an accountant or the tax office what records they expect you to keep, and for how long. That one conversation is worth more than any amount of guessing.

Who to ask

If you are unsure what counts, you have a few choices. A qualified accountant or tax adviser can look at your situation. Your local tax office usually has a helpline and website for general questions. Some countries offer free tax help for people on lower incomes. Use only official or qualified sources, and be wary of anyone who promises a big refund for a fee.

What counts as done

Done is receipts in one place, the three biggest photographed and a log with a few lines in it. You do not need to work out your tax. Write the number of receipts you gathered and the name of the person you will ask about the rest.

Then stop. Future you has a much easier afternoon waiting.
