How to build a household emergency folder when you keep putting it off

You know the folder you're supposed to have. The one with the policy numbers, the doctor's name, where the spare key is, who feeds the cat. You've thought about it for years, usually at 2am, and then the morning comes and it goes back on the pile. That isn't laziness. It's a job with no deadline, no reward and a slightly frightening premise, and brains that run on urgency don't start jobs like that.

The problem is that the week you need it is the week you can't make it. A hospital stay, a flood, a flight you can't get back from. Someone else is suddenly running your life, and they're guessing.

Why this is harder than it looks

An emergency folder sounds like one task. It's actually seven small ones hiding inside a scary name, and each one needs a different kind of looking. Account logins live in one place, medication doses in another, the gas shut-off is behind a cupboard you'd have to move. Try to do all of it in one sitting and you'll stall on the first missing policy number and close the laptop.

There's also the emotional weight. Writing down what happens if you're not there is uncomfortable, so the sensible move is to make each piece small enough that you're done before the discomfort catches up.

The week, one job a day

Twenty minutes a session, never more. Miss a day and you do it the next one, so the week can be nine days long and still count.

  1. Who to call. One page of names and numbers: next of kin, GP, insurer, landlord or mortgage lender, bank, employer. Add the account numbers you can find in twenty minutes and leave gaps for the rest.
  2. Where things are. Spare keys, the water stopcock, the fuse box, passports, the folder itself. A list, not a map. If you have to get up and look, that's the session.
  3. Medical. Conditions, medications and doses, allergies, pharmacy, the specialist's name. Copy it off the boxes in the bathroom cabinet.
  4. Money that keeps running. Which bills are on direct debit, which aren't, when rent or the mortgage goes out, and roughly how much needs to be in which account. The aim is that nothing bounces while you're away.
  5. Pets, plants, people and keys. Who feeds what and how often, who has a key, which neighbour knows you. Smaller than it sounds.
  6. The grab folder. Put the pages together somewhere physical. A plastic wallet by the door counts. If you'd rather it lived on a phone, a shared note works too.
  7. Tell two people. Pick the two people who'd be the ones to step in, and tell them where it is. Day seven is the one most people skip, and it's the one that makes the rest useful.

The words to say

Day seven is a short message, and the awkwardness of it is why people don't send it. Something in this spirit works:

> "Boring admin thing. I've put together a folder with my emergency contacts, meds and where everything is, in case I'm ever out of action. It's in the blue wallet in the kitchen drawer. You don't need to do anything, I just wanted someone to know."

If you're asking a neighbour about a key or a pet, keep it just as plain:

> "Would you be okay being the person who knows where my spare key is? Nothing to do unless something happens."

Nobody has ever minded being asked this. Mostly they're relieved someone's thought about it.

What counts as done

Done is not a perfect folder. Done is a folder that exists, in a known place, with two people who know it's there, and with gaps you'll fill in when you next come across the number. A half-filled page someone can find beats a complete one that only lives in your head. If you've got the who-to-call page and the medical page and nothing else, you're already far ahead of most households.
