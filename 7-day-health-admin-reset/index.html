There's a list in your head. The doctor's appointment you've been meaning to book. The dentist you haven't seen in a while. The repeat prescription that's about to run out. The eye test. The thing you've been meaning to get looked at. Each one is a phone call, and each phone call is the reason the list is still in your head and not in a diary.

That isn't neglect. Phone calls to receptionists are a specific kind of hard: you have to explain yourself out loud, to a stranger, while being asked questions you didn't prepare for, and the stakes feel high because it's your body. Avoiding that is a reasonable response. It just has a cost, and the cost grows.

Why this is harder than it looks

Health admin never arrives as one job. It's six or seven different organisations with different phone systems, different opening hours and different questions. The GP wants to know if it's urgent. The dentist wants to know when you were last in. The pharmacy wants a date of birth and a drug name you can't pronounce. Each call needs its own words, and improvising them under pressure is the part that stops you dialling.

Written down first, the call becomes reading aloud. That's a completely different task, and most people can do it even on a bad day.

The week, one job a day

One call or one chore a day. Nothing over twenty minutes. Miss a day and do it the next one; the week can run to nine days and still count.

  1. Monday: book the doctor. Ring at the quietest time you can manage, read the script, take whatever's offered.
  2. Tuesday: book the dentist. Same approach. If it's been years, say so plainly. They've heard longer.
  3. Wednesday: sort the prescription. Check what's left, request the repeat, note the collection date somewhere you'll see it.
  4. Thursday: book the eye test. Often the easiest call of the week. Many opticians book online, which counts.
  5. Friday: write the symptom list. Everything you've been meaning to mention, in plain words, with roughly when it started. Take it to Monday's appointment.
  6. Saturday: chase the referral. The one that went quiet. One call to ask where it is and what the wait looks like.
  7. Sunday: the one-page summary. Conditions, medications, allergies, who your GP is, dates of the appointments you just made. One page, kept where you can find it.

The words to say

For the GP receptionist:

"Hi, I'd like to book an appointment with a doctor please. It isn't an emergency. What's the earliest routine slot, and is there a cancellation list?"

For the dentist after a long gap:

"Hi, I'd like to register as a patient and book a check-up. It's been a while since I last saw a dentist, so I'd rather just get it booked."

For the referral that's gone quiet:

"Hi, I was referred to [department] on around [date] and haven't heard anything. Could you check where it's up to and roughly how long the wait is?"

Read them off the page. Nobody on the other end can tell.

What counts as done

Done is one appointment in the diary, one request sent, or one page written, depending on the day. Not the appointment attended, not the condition fixed. By Sunday you've got a GP slot, a dental slot, a prescription on its way, an eye test booked, a symptom list ready, a referral located and a summary page that makes every future call easier. A week ago all of that was a list in your head.
