The tax reminder arrives the week after the tax was due. The insurance auto-renews at a price you'd never have agreed to. The service light has been on since March. None of this is because you don't care about the car. It's because car admin is six separate annual deadlines, set by six different organisations, and not one of them lines up with another.

If you've been meaning to sort it for a year, you're not behind. You've just never been handed the dates in one place.

Why this is harder than it looks

Each deadline on its own is a small job. Finding the renewal date is five minutes. Comparing insurance quotes is twenty. Booking a service is one phone call. The hard part is that they're scattered across a year, each one only becomes visible when it's nearly late, and by the time it's urgent you're doing it in a panic with a fine attached.

The other trap is that the paperwork lives in four places: the glovebox, a kitchen drawer, an email from two years ago and an app you can't log into. Most of the stress of car admin is hunting, not doing.

The week, one job a day

Seven sessions, none over twenty minutes. Miss one and do it the next day; the week can be nine days long. The pack is written for whichever country you drive in, so you fill in your own agency names and dates.

  1. Tax and test dates. Find both. Your registration document, your government's vehicle site, or the last reminder letter will have them. Put them in your phone calendar with a reminder a month before and a week before.
  2. Insurance, and the switch. Find your renewal date and what you're paying. Set a reminder three weeks out, because that's when switching saves money. If renewal is close, run one comparison now.
  3. Book the service. One call or one web form. If you don't know a garage, ask one person you trust and go with their answer.
  4. The glovebox file. One envelope or wallet with the insurance certificate, breakdown details, a copy of the registration document and the service book. Everything you'd need at a roadside, in the car.
  5. The breakdown card. A single card with your breakdown number, policy number, registration and a short what-to-do list. Tape it inside the glovebox lid.
  6. The monthly five. A five-minute check you'll actually do: tyres, oil, lights, washer fluid, and a glance at the dashboard. Put a monthly reminder in.
  7. Sell or keep. An honest page. What the car costs you a year, what it would fetch, what's coming up. Not a decision, just the numbers in one place so the decision stops being a vague worry.

The words to say

Session two usually involves a phone call people dread, so here's the shape of it:

> "Hi, my renewal's coming up and I've had a cheaper quote elsewhere for the same cover. Before I switch, is there anything you can do on the price?"

Then stop talking. If they come down, good. If they don't, you've already got the other quote. For the garage booking:

> "I'd like to book a service. It's a [make and model], and I'm not sure when it was last done. What's your next available slot?"

You don't need to know anything else. They'll ask for what they need.

What counts as done

Done is every date in your calendar with a reminder before it, the documents in one place in the car, and a garage booking that exists. That's it. You don't have to have switched insurer or decided whether to sell. You've turned six surprises into six appointments, and surprises are what cost money.
