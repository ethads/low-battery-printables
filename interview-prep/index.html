How to prepare for a job interview when anxiety makes your mind go blank

A thirty-minute way to prepare for one interview: the facts, three real stories and words for the usual questions, plus what to do if you blank.

The email says yes. They would like to meet you. For about ten seconds you are pleased, and then the dread arrives. What if they ask something you can't answer? What if you freeze, and the one thing you remember is the thing you wish you had said?

Interview preparation advice usually tells you to research, rehearse, practise a hundred questions, and be authentic, all at once. That can feel like another job on top of finding the job. You do not need a hundred answers. You need about thirty minutes and a handful of real stories.

Prepared beats impressive

The goal of an interview is not to be dazzling. It is to give the people in the room enough real information to feel comfortable about hiring you. For that, short, honest, specific answers work better than long, polished ones. Aim for under two minutes each.

Nerves are not a flaw in this. A pause before you answer reads as thoughtful, not unsure. People who interview a lot have seen every kind of nervous and mostly want you to do well.

Step one: the basics (about eight minutes)

Open the job advert and a blank page. Write four short boxes.

  • The role: the job title and the three duties that come up most in the advert. Those are what they care about.
  • The place: what they do, in one sentence, and one thing you noticed on their website or in their news. This gives you something real to say when they ask why you want to work there.
  • Logistics: date, time, address or video link, who you ask for, and how long the journey takes. Add ten minutes. Check the route on a map, and if it is online, test the link and the sound tonight.
  • You: two skills that match the advert and one thing you are proud of. Use the advert's own words where you can.

Step two: your stories (about fifteen minutes)

Most questions that start with "tell me about a time when..." are asking for a story. You do not need to invent any, because you already have them. Pick three from your life, work or studies and write them in a three-part shape:

  1. The situation. One or two sentences about what was going on.
  2. What you did. The action you took, in plain words. Use "I", not "we".
  3. What happened. The result, or what you learned if it did not go perfectly.

Choose stories that cover the common themes: a problem you solved, a time you worked with other people, and a mistake you learned from. One story can often answer several questions if you shape it differently. Write each in a few lines only. Notes, not a script.

Step three: words to have ready (about seven minutes)

Three questions come up in nearly every interview. Write the beginning of an answer to each.

Tell me about yourself. "I'm a [job or background]. Most recently I [one thing]. I'm interested in this role because [reason]." Keep it to thirty seconds.

Why do you want to work here? "I like that you [specific thing from your research]. My experience in [skill] fits what the role needs."

Do you have any questions for us? Always have one or two. "What does a good first three months look like? And what happens next in the process?" This also tells you what the job is really like.

If you blank

It happens to nearly everyone. You have two clean exits:

"Can I take a moment to think about that?" Then actually take it. Three seconds of silence feels long to you and short to them.

"I'm not sure I have a perfect example. Could I tell you about something similar?" Then go to one of your three stories.

Neither is a failure. Both are ordinary interview behaviour, and both buy you time.

The night before and the morning of

Lay out the outfit, pack your bag with a copy of your CV, a pen and a notebook, and set an alarm. Say your answers out loud once. Saying them to the wall is the best practice there is, because it turns thoughts into sentences.

Eat something beforehand. A hungry brain blanks faster.

What counts as done

Done is not "I feel ready". You may never feel ready. Done is: the four boxes filled, three stories written, three answers started, outfit and route sorted. That is preparation. The rest is nerves, which come with the territory and can sit in the room with you.

After the interview, send a short thank-you note the same day. Thank them for their time and say one thing you enjoyed learning about the role. It takes three minutes and leaves a good last impression.
