What to do on a bad day when you can't decide anything

Some days arrive already heavy. You wake up and the list of things you "should" do is longer than your energy, and the idea of choosing between them is exhausting before you have even stood up.

Nothing is wrong with you for having a day like this. Everyone with a nervous system has them. People with ADHD, depression, anxiety, burnout or a new baby just have more of them, and the usual advice ("be kind to yourself", "do something small") quietly assumes you can still think of what the small thing is. On a bad day, you often can't.

The trick is to decide earlier

Choosing is the part that breaks first. Deciding what to eat, whether to shower, who to text, what counts as enough: each one costs energy you don't have. So the trick is to make the decisions on a better day and leave them somewhere you can find them.

Build the menu on a good day

Wait for a day with a little more energy. Not a perfect day, just an ordinary one. Sit down for ten minutes with a pen.

1. Write your four basics, in the smallest form you could manage.

  • Eat: the easiest thing in the house that counts as food. Toast, cereal, a banana, a tin of soup, a cheese sandwich. Write the actual items.
  • Drink: water, squash or tea. Write where the glass lives so you do not have to look for it.
  • Move: the tiniest version. Stand at a window, walk to the front door, stretch on the bed.
  • Wash: face, teeth, or a wipe-down. Choose whichever feels least like effort, and write that one.

2. Split each part of your day into two columns. A starter column and a main column. The starter is what you do on the worst days. The main is what you add when you have a bit more. For example:

  • Body: starter is water and one slice of toast. Main is a proper meal.
  • Home: starter is bin out or one cup to the sink. Main is a ten-minute tidy.
  • People: starter is a thumbs-up on a message. Main is a short call.
  • Screens: starter is a comfort show or a podcast. Main is a walk with headphones.
  • Something kind: starter is a blanket and a warm drink. Main is a bath or a favourite meal.

3. Write real answers, not ideal ones. If your honest starter for eating is crisps and a yoghurt, write that. The menu exists to get you through the day, not to impress anyone.

Words to have ready

Part of what makes a bad day worse is the effort of telling people. Pre-write the sentences, so that you can copy and paste or read them aloud.

To yourself: "Today is a bad day. I'm doing the starter menu and nothing else. That is enough."

To a friend or family member: "Rough day. Could you check in on me tonight, or drop round something easy to eat? You don't need to fix anything."

To work, kept short: "I'm not well today and need to take it easy. I'll pick this up as soon as I can."

On the day itself

Pull out the menu. Pick one thing from each starter row. Not all, one. Stop when you reach the bottom or when you run out, whichever comes first.

If you manage a main item, good. If you only manage the first line, that is also a full day. The aim of a bad day is not progress. It is not letting the basics slip so far that tomorrow is harder than today.

What counts as done

On a bad day, done is very small, and that is the point:

  • You ate something.
  • You drank something.
  • You told one person, even with a single word.

That is it. If you managed all three, you did what the day needed. Everything else is a bonus and can be ignored without guilt.

A note on safety

A bad-day menu is a tool for low-energy days. It is not a treatment, and it does not replace help. If bad days are becoming most days, or if you are having thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to your doctor or a local crisis line, or contact your local emergency number. Asking for help is part of looking after yourself, not a failure of the menu.
