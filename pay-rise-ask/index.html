How to ask for a pay rise when the thought of it makes you freeze

How to prepare and ask for a pay rise with evidence, a number and a short script, including what to say if the answer is not now.

Quick test. Has your job grown since your pay last changed? More tasks, more people relying on you, a bigger piece of the work? If yes, you may be doing more for the same money, and you are not alone. Most people wait to be offered a raise. It rarely arrives on its own.

The reason many of us do not ask is not greed or fear of the answer. It is that the conversation feels like a confrontation, and nobody wants to be the person who "makes it awkward". This guide takes the awkwardness out by turning the ask into a short preparation job.

A different way to see it

You are not asking for a favour. You are starting a normal business conversation about whether your pay matches the work you do. Managers expect it. A calm request with facts is usually received better than silent resentment that comes out sideways later.

The prep, in four boxes

Give yourself about half an hour, and write on paper.

What has changed. List the extra tasks, the bigger scope or the added responsibility since your last pay change. Be specific. "Training the two new starters" is better than "more work".

Your wins. Three results, with numbers where you can. Time saved, a client kept, an error fixed, a project delivered early. Check old emails, reviews and messages. Praise from colleagues or clients counts, so screenshot it.

The market rate. Look at job adverts and salary guides for similar roles in your area and write down a range. Several sources are better than one.

Your number. Pick a figure at the upper end of a fair range, not the middle. If you aim for the middle, you will land below it.

Put your wins in a small log: what you did, when, and the result. Memory is bad at this, and a page of evidence makes you steadier in the room.

Book the meeting first

Do not ambush your manager in a corridor. Send a message:

"Hi [name], could we find 20 minutes this week to talk about my role and pay?"

That is a complete message. You do not need to explain more. Sending it is the first, and hardest, step.

What to say in the meeting

Read from your notes if you need to.

"Since [date], my role has grown to include [list]. I have [your wins]. Based on that, and what similar roles pay, I would like to discuss moving to [number]."

Then stop talking. The pause will feel long. Let it. The silence is where they think.

If the answer is "not now"

This is common, and it is not the end.

"What would I need to show to get there, and can we review it in [three months]?"

Ask for a specific target and a date. Then follow up in writing the same day: "Thanks for meeting today. To confirm, we agreed that I will [targets] and we will review on [date]." A written note keeps it from fading.

If the answer is a flat no with no path, you have learned something useful about the job, and you can decide what to do with it.

What counts as done

Done is not "I got the raise". Done is "I booked the meeting". If you have sent the message and filled in your wins, you have done the hard part. The conversation itself is shorter than the dread.
