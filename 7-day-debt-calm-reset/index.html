The envelopes are in a drawer. Or the emails sit unopened under a number you have stopped looking at. You know they are there. You have a rough idea of who they are from, and the idea alone is enough to make the drawer stay shut.

Avoiding debt letters is one of the most common things people do, and it is not stupidity or laziness. The letters feel like a verdict. Opening one risks a bad number, a bad tone or both. So your brain, trying to protect you, files the whole thing under "later". The trouble is that later does not make the pile smaller.

Why it feels so heavy

Debt is not one problem. It is a bundle: who you owe, how much, when it is due, what happens if you miss it and what you could do about it. Your head tries to hold all of that at once, which is why the whole thing feels like a wall.

Split the wall into bricks and each brick is small. Opening an envelope is a brick. Writing a name on a list is a brick. None of them needs you to be brave for more than a few minutes.

The week, one brick a day

Nothing here takes longer than twenty minutes, and you are not deciding how to pay anything off. You are getting the facts together so you can ask for proper help from a calm place.

  1. Monday: open the post. Put every unopened letter in one box. Open each one and note who it is from, the date and the biggest number. Stop at ten minutes.
  2. Tuesday: write the list. One page, one line per debt: who, what for, roughly how much. Blanks are fine.
  3. Wednesday: find the numbers. Add the balance, the minimum payment and the due date for each. Add up the minimums.
  4. Thursday: see what is due first. Put the nearest date at the top. Circle anything due in the next two weeks.
  5. Friday: book free help. Look up free, independent debt advice in your country and book a slot or send an enquiry. This is the big one.
  6. Saturday: one short message. Pick one creditor and ask what options they have. Do not agree to anything yet.
  7. Sunday: write the one page. Pull the list, the appointment and the call notes together, and put them in one bag.

The words to say

When you do not know what a letter is about:

"Hi, I've received a letter from you with reference [number]. Could you tell me what this relates to and what the balance is?"

When you are ringing about a payment:

"Hi, I'm having trouble keeping up with my payments on account [number]. Could you tell me what options you have for people in this situation?"

When someone presses you to agree to something:

"Thank you. I'd like to take advice before I agree to anything. Could you note my account and put the details in writing?"

That last one is a complete sentence. You do not owe anyone a decision on the spot.

Free help is a real thing

Most countries have free, independent, non-profit or government-backed debt advice services. Search for "free debt advice" and your country, and choose one that does not charge you. Be careful of anyone who asks for money upfront, promises to wipe your debt or tells you to stop talking to your creditors without explanation. A real adviser will look at your whole situation and tell you your options, including ones you may not have known about.

Advisers have heard every story. You will not shock them, and they do not judge.

What counts as done

On Monday, done is an opened pile. On Friday, done is an appointment or an enquiry sent. By Sunday, done is one page and one bag, ready for the adviser. You do not need to have a plan to pay anything. You need to know where you stand and to have asked for help.

If a day slips, do it the next day. The week can be nine days long.
