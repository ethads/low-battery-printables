How to prepare for an adult ADHD assessment when you don't know what to bring

A calm week of preparation for an adult ADHD assessment: what to write down, who to ask and what to bring. Preparation only, not a test or advice.

A quick note first. This is about getting ready for an appointment. It cannot tell you whether you have ADHD, and it doesn't replace a qualified professional. That's their job, not yours and not an article's.

Now the human part. You've booked the assessment, or you are about to. The date is weeks away and part of you is already panicking, because the appointment will ask you to describe years of what has been hard, in an hour, to someone you've never met. And your mind, as it always does under pressure, will go blank.

Here's a reassuring thing to know. The people who run these appointments don't expect a perfect speech. They want specific, ordinary examples. And the best way to have those ready is to write them down now, a bit at a time.

What helps most

Assessors tend to listen for real, repeated, everyday examples more than for labels. "I missed three bill due dates this year because I didn't open the post" is more useful than "I'm disorganised". It's evidence, and you already have plenty. The aim of the week is to get it out of your head and onto paper.

The week, one job a day

Twenty minutes at most. If a day slips, do it the next one.

  1. Monday: get the logistics straight. Date, time, place or video link, forms that have to come back, and who you can bring. Put a reminder in your phone.
  2. Tuesday: write your real examples. Four areas: attention and focus, organisation and time, restlessness or impulsivity, mood and energy. One or two examples each from the last six months, with how often and what it costs you.
  3. Wednesday: look for childhood evidence. Old school reports, certificates, teacher comments, photos of paperwork. And what you remember. If you find very little, write "none found". That is fine.
  4. Thursday: ask someone who knows you. A parent, partner or close friend. A short, kind message asking for a few examples.
  5. Friday: the practical page. Current medicines with doses, other diagnoses, your doctor's name, sleep, caffeine, alcohol and recent big life changes.
  6. Saturday: write your questions. What you want from the appointment, and three to six things to ask.
  7. Sunday: pack the folder. Everything in one place, by the door, with a reminder for the morning.

Words you can borrow

Asking someone who knows you:

"Hi, I've got an assessment coming up for attention and focus. Would you mind jotting down a few things you've noticed about me over the years, or what I was like as a kid? Even a few lines helps."

To the service, if something is unclear:

"Hi, I have an assessment booked on [date]. Could you confirm what I need to bring and whether there are forms to fill in beforehand?"

For your questions in the room:

"What will happen after today, and how long should I expect to wait?"

"If the outcome isn't what I expected, what are my options?"

Asking for a repeat of anything is fine, and so is asking for the outcome in writing.

If you can't remember much

This is more common than people think. Childhood memories fade, records get lost, and parents aren't always available. A thin folder doesn't count against you. Say so in the appointment: "I don't have many records, but here's what I remember."

What counts as done

Done on Tuesday is a page of examples in your own words. Done on Sunday is one folder, packed. You don't have to prove anything. You are collecting what you'd tell a professional, so you can say it clearly instead of searching for it under stress.

On the day, you don't have to say it perfectly. You have it written down.

Looking after yourself

Appointments can be tiring, even good ones. Plan a small kind thing for afterwards, bring water and a snack, and write down what was said within an hour, because it fades fast. Whatever the outcome, it is one step, not the whole story.
