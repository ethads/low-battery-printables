How to sort a doom box when you can't face organising anything

A fifteen-minute way to sort the box of random stuff using four piles and one verdict per thing, with no organising system and the lid back on after.

There's a box. Maybe a drawer, maybe a bag, maybe a corner. It's full of things that didn't have an obvious home at the time, so they went in the box, and now the box is a place you don't open. Chargers for devices you no longer own. A birthday card you meant to send. Three pens, a receipt, a key to something.

People with ADHD call it a doom box, and the "doom" is sometimes taken to stand for "didn't organise, only moved". The box isn't a failure. It's a reasonable response to having too many small decisions and not enough energy to make them. Each thing in there was one decision you postponed, and now there are eighty of them stacked in a shoebox, which is why opening it feels like being handed eighty jobs at once.

Why this is harder than it looks

"Just sort the box" assumes you know where things go. Mostly you don't, and that's why they're in the box. So you pick up a cable, don't know what it's for, don't know whether to keep it, and put it back. Twenty minutes later you've touched everything and sorted nothing.

The other trap is organising. You start sorting and your brain jumps to "I need little containers for this" or "I should label these", and suddenly a fifteen-minute job needs a trip to the shops. That's how the lid goes back on with everything still inside.

Here's the trick that makes it manageable: everything in the box survived being ignored, so nothing in it is urgent. You are sorting, not organising. And when the timer rings, the lid goes back on whatever's left.

The fifteen-minute version

Set a timer for fifteen minutes. Tip the box out onto a table or the floor. Have a bin bag within reach.

  1. Four piles, fast. Has a home. Needs a decision. Paper. Bin. Pick each thing up once and drop it in a pile. Don't think about where the home is yet, just whether one exists.
  2. Bin goes in the bin bag now. Dead batteries, the cable to nothing, the receipt from 2019. This is the easiest pile and it shrinks the mess fastest.
  3. Paper gets one glance each. Is it a thing you need to act on, or a thing you can recycle? Act-on paper goes in one spot. The rest is recycling.
  4. For the "needs a decision" pile, give each thing one verdict. Keep, give, sell, bin. One word, written next to the thing on a list. Don't deliberate. Your first answer is almost always right.
  5. Be honest about "sell". Selling is a trap unless you'll list it within a week. If you won't, it's "give" or "bin". A thing waiting to be sold is just a thing in a different box.
  6. When the timer rings, stop. Lid on anything left. You're allowed to do this again another day.

The words to say

Offering something to a friend is the fastest way to get it out of the house, and it feels better than binning it.

"Hey, I'm clearing out and I've got a [thing] I don't use. Do you want it? No worries at all if not, I'll just pass it on."

For the charity bag, the trick is a deadline where you can see it. Write "out by Saturday" on a sticky note and stick it to the bag. A bag with a date on it leaves the house. A bag without one lives by the door for a month.

What counts as done

Done is the "has a home" pile walked to its homes, five things max. Not every verdict acted on. Not a tidy box with dividers. Five things back where they live, the bin bag out, and the lid back on the rest. That's a sorted box, even if it's not an empty one, and the next fifteen minutes will be easier because the bin pile is already gone. Walk the "has a home" pile to its homes, five things max. Lid on the rest.
