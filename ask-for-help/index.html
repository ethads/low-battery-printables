How to ask someone for help when you do not know how to start

A practical guide to asking for help when you are overwhelmed, how to shrink the request, pick the right person and use words that get a yes.

Some jobs are stuck, not because they are hard, but because you have been trying to do them alone.

The pile of post. The phone call to the bank. The kitchen that you have been meaning to tackle since February. You know a second person in the room would make it easier. You also cannot imagine saying so, because the sentence "I need help" feels huge and slightly embarrassing.

Here is a different way in. It starts from a small fact: most people like being asked for a specific, small thing, and many feel quietly pleased to be trusted with it.

Why asking feels harder than the job

Asking for help brings up a lot at once. You worry about being a burden, about being judged, about owing someone back, about getting a yes that comes with a sigh. And "help me" is also vague, so the other person does not know what you want, and may offer advice when you wanted company.

That vagueness is fixable. The smaller and more specific the ask, the easier it is to say and to answer.

Shrink it to one small part

Take five minutes, with a pen.

  1. Name the job in one sentence. "The pile of post." "Ringing the bank."
  2. Find the smallest piece someone else could do or sit through with you. Not the whole job. "Sit with me for twenty minutes while I open the post." "Stay on the phone while I make the call."
  3. Pick a day and a time. Offer one. People say yes to a specific time much more often than to "sometime".
  4. Pick how. In the room, on a call, by text, or just being nearby. Body-doubling, where someone quietly keeps you company while you work, helps many people start.

Choose who to ask

Write four names, ordered by who would be easiest to ask, not who would be best. A friend, a family member, a neighbour, a colleague. Include one person who is not close family: variety makes it less loaded.

If a name makes your chest tighten, put them lower down. There is no rule that says you must ask the hardest person first.

Words to copy

To a friend or family member:

"Hey, I am stuck on [the job] and could use a hand. Could you [the smallest part] on [day]? It would take about [time]."

For body-doubling:

"Could you stay on a call while I do [the job]? You do not need to do anything. It just helps me start."

At work:

"I am struggling to get [task] done. Could we find fifteen minutes to talk it through?"

If they say they cannot, say "No worries, thanks anyway", then send the same message to the next name. A no is about their week, not about you.

Say thank you and offer something back

After the help, a thank you matters. Mention what it meant: "That made the post feel doable." If you can, offer something in return, even a coffee. It keeps the exchange easy for next time.

When a favour is not enough

Sometimes the stuck feeling is bigger than a pile of tasks. If everything feels too heavy for a long while, it is worth talking to a doctor or a local support service. Asking them is the same kind of ask, just with a professional.

What counts as done

Done is not "the job is finished". Done is "I asked one person". If they said yes, put the date in your phone. If they said no, send the message to the second name. The job gets easier once somebody else knows about it.
