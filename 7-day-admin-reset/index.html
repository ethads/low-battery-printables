It's been months. The envelopes have stopped being individual letters and become a texture on the side table. The appointment you meant to book is now the appointment you should have had. You've got good at not looking, and the not-looking has started to take up more room than the admin ever would.

That isn't laziness. Avoidance is what a tired brain does with jobs that have no clear end and a bad feeling attached. The longer you leave them, the worse the feeling, and the better the reasons to leave them a bit longer. The trick isn't willpower. It's making the next step small enough that the bad feeling doesn't get a vote.

Why this is harder than it looks

Catching up sounds like one project. It isn't. It's a phone call, a login, a letter, a reply, a form and a trip to the post office, each needing a different kind of courage and a different set of words. Trying to do them in one sitting means finding all of that courage at once, and the sitting ends after the first job, usually with the rest feeling worse than before.

The other problem is scoring. Without a line that says "this is done", every job feels unfinished, so nothing ever feels like progress. You need a plan that names one job per day, caps the time, and tells you exactly when you're allowed to stop.

The week, one job a day

Twenty minutes is the ceiling for every day. If you miss a day, do it the next one. A nine-day week counts as a week.

  1. Monday: mail. Open every envelope without fixing anything. Find the one that matters and put it on top.
  2. Tuesday: bank. Open the app, read the balance, write it down, close the app. Looking is the job, not fixing.
  3. Wednesday: subscriptions. Go down last month's statement and circle anything recurring. Cancel the one you'd forgotten.
  4. Thursday: doctor. Ring and book. The words are below, so you can read them off the page.
  5. Friday: replies. Three messages, short, no apology paragraph. Send them and close the app.
  6. Saturday: forms. Gather the numbers you'll need, answer five questions, save. Finishing isn't the job.
  7. Sunday: returns. Label on, parcel bagged, dropped off. Out of the hallway for good.

Then a last page for what came back: money found, appointments made, replies received, and the one or two changes that stop the pile rebuilding.

The words to say

The phone call is where most of these jobs die. Having a script in front of you means you don't have to think of the words while your heart is going. For the receptionist:

"Hi, I'd like to book a routine appointment with a GP please. I'm flexible on days. What's the earliest you've got?"

For a bill you can't pay in full right now:

"Hi, I've got a bill due on [date] and I can't cover all of it this month. Can we set up a payment plan, or move the date?"

For the reply you've put off so long it feels impossible:

"Sorry this took me a while. Yes, that works. Thanks for waiting on me."

Short, polite, no backstory. Nobody needs the backstory.

What counts as done

Done is the one thing named for that day, nothing more. Mail opened. Balance written down. One subscription cancelled. Appointment booked. Three replies sent. Five questions saved. Parcel gone. If you stop at that line every day, by Sunday you've done seven real jobs, which after months of avoidance is a lot. The point of the line is that you're allowed to cross it and then stop.
