It's seven in the evening and you haven't eaten properly today. The fridge has condiments and half a lemon. Ordering in feels expensive and also somehow like too many steps. You end up eating cereal standing up, or nothing, and promising yourself tomorrow will be different.

That is not a willpower problem. Feeding yourself involves about a dozen decisions a day, every day, forever, and decisions are exactly what run out first when your energy is low. The answer isn't to try harder. It's to make the decisions once, in advance, so the hungry version of you has nothing to decide.

Why this is harder than it looks

Meal planning advice is written for people with energy. It assumes you'll follow recipes, shop for specific ingredients, and cook something new each night. On a flat day that's three obstacles before you've eaten. What low-energy eating actually needs is defaults: a short list of meals you can make without thinking, from things that are always in the house.

The other thing nobody plans for is the floor day, when even the defaults are too much. If the plan has no answer for that day, the plan fails on the day you need it most.

The week, one job a day

Nothing takes more than twenty minutes. Miss a day and you do it the next one; the week can be nine days long.

  1. Pick the five defaults. Five meals you already know how to make, like, and can do half-asleep. Beans on toast counts. Pasta with a jar of sauce counts. Write them down. These are what you eat most nights now, and that's fine.
  2. The one list. Write one shopping list that covers all five defaults plus breakfast and snacks. This is the list you buy from every time, so you never have to write another.
  3. The shop. Buy the list. Online delivery if that's easier. The job is to get the ingredients into the house, not to enjoy the supermarket.
  4. The backstops. Stock the freezer and cupboard with things that become a meal with no effort: frozen veg, frozen dumplings, tinned soup, microwave rice, eggs. These are for the nights the defaults feel like too much.
  5. The ten-minute list. Write down everything you can make in ten minutes or less from what's now in the house. Stick it on the fridge, so the hungry you can just read it.
  6. The snack shelf. One shelf or box with food that needs no preparation at all. Nuts, crackers, fruit, cheese, yoghurt. Eating something beats eating nothing.
  7. The floor days. Decide now what happens when nothing works: which takeaway, which backstop, which snack. Write it down. A floor day with a plan is just a day.

The words to say

The scripts here are mostly for yourself. When you're standing at the fridge at seven o'clock: "Which of the five?" If none of them, "Which backstop?" If still nothing, "Snack shelf, then bed." That's the whole decision tree, and it ends in food every time.

If someone offers to help and you don't know what to ask for: "Could you pick up the stuff on this list? It's always the same list." Hand them the one list. That's it.

What counts as done

Done is: five defaults written down, one shopping list, the shop done, backstops in the freezer and cupboard, a ten-minute list on the fridge, a snack shelf that exists, and a written floor-day plan. Done is not eating well every day. It's having a system that means you eat something every day, including the bad ones. No recipes to follow, no diet, no rules about what counts as proper food.
