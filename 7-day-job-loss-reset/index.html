What to do in the first week after losing your job

Nobody tells you how much paperwork a job ending creates. In the same week you are dealing with the shock, you are also being asked for forms, dates, final pay details, benefit questions and the dreaded "so what will you do now?" from relatives.

If you are reading this a few days after losing a job, take a breath. You do not have to have a plan this week. You do not have to be fine. You only need seven small things done, one at a time, so the next month is less chaotic.

What the first week is for

The first week after a job ends is for protecting yourself. Not for finding the next job, not for deciding who you are now. Protecting means knowing the facts, keeping your papers, knowing your money, finding out what help exists and telling a few people.

Everything else can wait. Rest counts too.

One task a day

Each takes twenty minutes at most.

  1. Day one: write the facts. The date you were told, the date it ends, who told you, and what was said about pay, notice and leave. Put the page in a folder.
  2. Day two: gather your papers. Your contract, payslips, letters and the contact details of people who might give a reference. Save personal copies of your own papers while you still have access.
  3. Day three: list what goes out. Your balance, what you expect from your old job and everything leaving each month. This is the number your fear has been inflating.
  4. Day four: find out what support exists. Look up your country's official employment or government service and read the eligibility page. Note any deadlines.
  5. Day five: tell three people. One kind person, one who may know of work, one good company. A short message each.
  6. Day six: set up the next steps. One line about the work you would like, one job alert, and a date to start searching.
  7. Day seven: write the one page. Dates, money, support, people and one gentle thing for tomorrow.

Words you can use

To get the details in writing:

"Hi [name], thank you for speaking with me. Could you please confirm in writing my end date, my final pay and any leave or payments owed?"

To ask for a reference:

"Hi [name], I'm leaving [company] and I'm starting to look for something new. Would you be willing to be a reference for me? I can send you a short summary of what I've been doing."

To a friend:

"Hi [name], I lost my job this week. I'm okay but it's been a lot. Would you be up for a chat or a coffee this week?"

These are polite requests, not disputes. If you think something is wrong with your final pay or notice, ask your country's official workplace body or a free advice service, because the rules vary by place and employer.

Do not sign under pressure

If you are handed something to sign, such as a settlement or an agreement, it is fine to say you need time to read it. "Thank you, I'll read this properly and come back to you" is polite and complete. If it matters, you can ask a union, a free legal advice service or a qualified adviser to look first.

Looking after yourself

A job ending is a real loss, even when it was not a good job. You may feel numb, angry, relieved or all three in a morning. That is normal. If the low feeling gets heavy or lasts, talk to your doctor or a local support service. You do not have to wait until it feels bad enough.

What counts as done

Done on day one is a page of facts. Done on day four is a support option with a deadline in your phone. By day seven, done is one page you can pick up when you feel foggy. You do not need a new job. You need a steadier footing, and this gives you that.

If a day slips, do it the next day. The week can be nine days long.
