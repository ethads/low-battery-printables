How to plan Christmas when the admin makes you dread December

A week of short jobs to get gifts, money, cards, travel and food out of your head and onto one page, so December feels lighter. Works for any holiday.

Christmas has a particular kind of weight, and it isn't the day itself. It is the pile of small decisions beforehand. Who to buy for. How much to spend. What to cook, where to be, when to post. Each one is minor. Together they sit on your chest from about the first of December.

If that's you, the problem isn't that you're bad at Christmas. It is that all of it is living in your head at once, with no edges. The fix is to give it edges: get it out onto paper and cut it into jobs you can do in twenty minutes.

Start with what you want

Before any list, a question that most plans skip: what would you like this December to feel like?

Write three things you want. "A quiet day." "See my sister." "Not be broke in January."

Then write three things you dread. "The big family meal." "Posting parcels." "Buying for the whole work team."

Circle one from each list. The first is your priority. The second is your first candidate for the "not this year" pile. Doing this on day one saves you from spending the week perfecting things you didn't care about.

The week, one job a day

Twenty minutes maximum each. A nine-day week is allowed.

  1. Monday: what do you want? Three wants, three dreads, one of each circled.
  2. Tuesday: list the people. Everyone Christmas touches, with a plan beside each name: gift, card, message or nothing. It always feels like fifty. It's usually fifteen.
  3. Wednesday: set the money. A total you can spend without worrying, rounded down. Split it, then put a number beside each name and adjust until it fits.
  4. Thursday: plan the gifts. One idea per person, grouped by shop. Check delivery dates. Buy two or three and stop.
  5. Friday: cards, post and wrapping. Look up your postal service's last posting dates and add three days. Decide whether cards are a yes. Put the dates in your phone as alarms.
  6. Saturday: food and travel. Where you'll be, how you're getting there, who's cooking what, and any allergies.
  7. Sunday: the one page. Wants, total, key dates, three jobs left. On the fridge. Done.

Words that take the pressure off

Suggesting a smaller scale to family or friends:

"Hi all, I'm keeping Christmas simple this year. Could we do a secret Santa, or just the kids, so it's less stress?"

Asking for gift ideas:

"Is there anything you'd like this year? Something small is fine, I'd just like to get it right."

If you're a guest:

"I can bring a dessert and something to drink. Could someone tell me the numbers and any allergies?"

And to set a limit on your own energy:

"I'll be there from two to five, and I'm going to need a quiet break in the afternoon."

You'll often find someone replies with relief. People are frequently dreading the same thing.

A word on money

Spend a number that you can afford in January too. This is not financial advice, just a way to begin: write the total down, and treat it as a ceiling. Handmade things, time together and a card with a few honest lines are all real gifts. Nobody is keeping score the way you fear.

What counts as done

Done on Tuesday is a list of names on one page. Done on Friday is a set of posting dates in your phone. By Sunday, done is a single page you wrote yourself, with a "not this year" list at the bottom. You are not aiming for a perfect Christmas. You're aiming for a manageable one that you can actually enjoy a bit.

In the final week before the holiday, only do the dated things: posting, food orders and travel. Everything else can wait, or go.

If your holiday isn't Christmas

Swap the words and keep the shape. The same seven jobs work for any end-of-year holiday, or any big family occasion with gifts, food and travel attached.
