How to sort out your medicine cabinet when you don't know what's expired

A ten-minute sort of old tablets, creams and first aid supplies, with a safe place for old medicines to go and the words to ask a pharmacist.

Somewhere in your house is a drawer or a shelf where medicines go to be forgotten. A packet of painkillers from two houses ago. A cream with no label. Half a course of something you stopped taking. Eye drops that are definitely older than they should be.

You know you should sort it. It's one of those jobs that is never urgent until suddenly you need something at 3am and can't find it, or can't tell whether it's still okay to use. Then you feel bad. So the drawer stays shut.

This article is practical, not medical. It can help you sort and tidy. It can't tell you what's safe to take or how much. Questions like that belong with a pharmacist or doctor.

Before you start

Pick a surface and clear it. A table or the kitchen counter works. Take everything out of the drawer, shelf or bag and spread it out in front of you. It looks worse than before. That's temporary.

Set a timer for ten minutes. You're checking dates and removing the obvious, not making health decisions.

Sort into four groups

Pick up each item and put it in one of four piles:

  1. Painkillers. Tablets, liquids, gels. Check each date.
  2. Prescriptions. Anything with your name on it. Keep what you still use, and set aside anything you've finished or stopped.
  3. Creams, drops and ointments. These go off sooner than tablets. Eye drops especially often have a short life once opened. Many tubes have a small symbol showing how many months they last after opening, so look for that.
  4. First aid. Plasters, bandages, a thermometer, antiseptic. Check that it's all still there and still sealed.

Write each item on a list with its expiry date. A simple table works: item, expiry, keep, and return or replace.

What goes where

Anything out of date goes in a bag to return. So does anything with no label, anything you can't identify, and anything you've been told to stop taking. When you can't remember what it was for, set it aside. Don't guess.

Medicines that are in date and clearly yours can go back. Keep them in the box they came in, so that the leaflet and the date stay with them.

If you need to replace something, write it on a list. A new packet of plasters or a fresh thermometer battery is much easier to buy when it's written down.

Getting rid of old medicines

It's best not to put old medicines in the bin or down the toilet, because it can harm people and the environment. Most pharmacies will take unwanted medicines back, or the equivalent service in your area does. Ring or ask at the counter:

"Hi, I've got some old medicines to hand in. Is this the right place, and do I need to do anything first?"

If you're unsure whether something is safe to keep, or what it was for, you can say:

"Can you tell me if this is still okay to use, and what I should do if I'm not sure?"

Pharmacists are used to this question. It's a normal thing to ask, and nobody will judge you for the state of your drawer.

Keep medicines safe

A few simple habits make this easier next time. Keep them somewhere cool and dry, which means the bathroom is often not ideal. Keep them out of the reach of children. Keep them in their original packaging with the leaflet. And if you live with others, make sure someone knows where the first aid supplies are.

What counts as done

Done is a bag by the front door for the pharmacy, a short list of things to replace, and a drawer with only in-date items in it. That's the whole job. If you only got through the painkillers, that counts, and you can do the creams another day.

Put the return bag with your keys so it leaves the house with you. Wipe the empty shelf. Put the date of your next quick check in your phone, six months from now, with a note that says: "Check dates, 5 minutes."
